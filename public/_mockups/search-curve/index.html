What you are choosing. All three cover the same distance in the same time. The curve decides where inside that time the distance gets spent, and that is what reads as fast or slow, not the duration number.

A is what ships today. It is only 29% travelled at 100 ms, so the sheet takes a visible beat to answer the finger, then moves evenly. Nothing about it looks unfinished, it just does not feel caused by the tap.

B is Airbnb's own curve, read off your recording frame by frame. It is 81% travelled at 100 ms and 80% done by 96 ms, so it spends the last 271 ms of its 367 ms creeping through the final fifth. That creep is what reads as too fast, because the motion looks finished long before it actually is.

C is the control point midpoint of A and B, so it sits between them by construction rather than by taste. It is 57.5% travelled at 100 ms, which puts the answer to the tap inside the window where a response still feels caused by the finger, and it does not reach 80% until 147 ms, so it never parks in B's looks-finished state. The cost, named: C spends 60% of its clock inside the last fifth of the travel, against A's 48%. Front loading always buys the finger an answer and pays for it with a longer near-settled tail. If that tail bothers you, the lever is the duration, not the curve.

Where the numbers come from. The times on each label are the curve's own, exact. They were then checked against this page: the loop was frozen and each frame painted at a chosen instant, which gives at 100 ms a travel of 29% for A, 81% for B and 57.5% for C, and free-running rAF sampling at 60 Hz puts every crossing within one frame of its label.

Two honest gaps in this page. The scrim here is a flat tint rather than the live blurred backdrop, so three stages can hold 60 fps and the timings stay trustworthy. The sheet is an empty white box, because its contents do not change between the three candidates and would only distract from the shape of the morph.
