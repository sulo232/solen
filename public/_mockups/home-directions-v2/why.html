Why apps choose what they choose

Four questions every booking home screen has to answer, the positions real apps take on each, the belief sitting under each position, and what each one costs. Where two credible apps do opposite things, both sides stay standing. Nothing on this page is a count of how many apps do a thing, because a count tells you what is common and cannot tell you why.

Question one of four

Does the screen ask, or show?

When somebody arrives, does the product ask them to state what they want, or show them what there is? The real axis is not intent versus curiosity. It is how much of the query the screen forces you to declare before it will show you anything, and who pays for that. And the variable that separates the two camps is population, not mood: asking first answers "who are you and what do you do", showing first answers "welcome back".

1

The segmented bar

THE BELIEF. A booking query is not one string, it is several fields at once, and a result set built from half a query is a promise the product will have to break. So the control names every dimension up front, and every field is a bounded picker rather than a text box, which makes the query valid by construction: there is no typo that returns zero.

Fresha web ships treatment, location, date and time-of-day. Its fourth field is not an end date, it is "Any time", because an appointment is a point with a preferred window. And the treatment field's dropdown IS the category list.

WHAT IT BUYS

It teaches the query language before you type a character: you now know this product indexes by treatment, place, date and time. It front-loads the constraints that decide whether a result can exist at all, rather than filtering a list you then have to shrink. And it becomes a receipt: Fresha carries the filled bar into the results header, so a user can read and edit their own state without a mental model of which filter they set.

WHAT IT COSTS

The whole top zone, and on Fresha web the whole first viewport: the first salon photograph is below the fold in every single capture. Four empty fields read as four decisions, three of which a returning customer already answered six weeks ago. It cannot survive onto a phone unchanged. And it hides the taxonomy: somebody who does not know the word "Barbering" has to open a field to learn that barbering exists.

TWO DETAILS WORTH STEALING. Airbnb's bar changes arity with its own state: once dates exist, the two date segments collapse into one reading "When: Weekend in June", and the round icon button grows into a labelled Search pill. And Airbnb Experiences ships three segments, not four, because an experience happens on a day and a stay spans a range. The number of fields is dictated by the product's time shape, not by a house style.

Suits a product whose query has irreducible dimensions, where a wrong tuple wastes a real search and the visitor may be new enough to need the vocabulary taught. Harms a short-catalogue, high-repeat product: if the same tuple is submitted every six weeks, the bar is a form the user fills out to tell the product something it already knows.

2

The collapsed pill, in two sub-species that matter more than the shape they share

THE BELIEF. One question at a time, because a phone only has room for one. Behind Airbnb's "Start your search" pill is a stacked accordion holding the same four fields: the answered step collapses to a one-line row with its value right-aligned, the active step is an expanded card, and the unanswered steps wait below. The segmented bar and the accordion are one concept under two space budgets: side by side where width is free, stacked where it is not.

2a is a prompt ("Start your search", no state). 2b is a summary of the live query: Fresha iOS packs three values into one pill over a map, the treatment, then "Tomorrow", then a truncated "Current location", and Airbnb's results pill runs two lines, "Homes in New York City" over the dates and the guest count.

The rule both apps obey, and it is the useful finding here: the entry control shows the query when a query exists and an invitation when it does not. Airbnb is 2a on home and 2b on results. Fresha iOS is 2b from the first tap because it enters through a map that already carries a default query. Neither ever shows an invitation over live state, and neither shows state that is empty.

Buys one row instead of a hero, so real content is in the first viewport, plus genuine progressive disclosure: one question on screen means each question gets a real picker instead of a cramped dropdown. Costs a tap before you learn the query language, and in the 2a form it tells a returning user nothing about their own state. Harms a one-dimensional query: a pill concealing a single text field charges a tap for nothing.

3

The intent grid. There is no query, there is a menu.

THE BELIEF. The catalogue is bounded and nameable, so a text field is the wrong instrument: it asks the user to guess vocabulary the product already owns. Show the vocabulary instead.

Look at the order on Square Go: a wordless grey search field first, a magnifier glyph with no placeholder text at all, measured 51 pt, then the headline "Schedule your next appointment", then a grid of 3D category tiles. The field is present, small, and says nothing. And when it goes sticky the feed heading reads "Recommended in 94025". Location was never asked for. It was resolved silently and then proven in the section title, which is a real technique: instead of a location field, a location claim the user can check.

Hers has no search field in its first viewport at all: eight goal tiles, and the eighth is "Browse all treatments" carrying the magnifier, so search is demoted to a member of the grid that replaced it. Fresha iOS runs fourteen tiles and no search control on home whatsoever.

Buys zero typing, zero vocabulary risk, and merchandising for free, because a tile with a photograph of nails sells the category while it navigates. It also answers the first-timer's actual first question, which is not "where is search" but "what can I even book here". Costs vertical space, and a hard ceiling: nothing in a grid can say "Tuesday evening near the station". It also silently declares a taxonomy, so whatever is not a tile does not exist for a browsing user. Harms a long-tail catalogue, and any query whose binding constraint is time, which is exactly why Square Go keeps the field, wordless, above the grid.

4

Search demoted to a utility. A returning user does not type.

THE BELIEF. The query is picked, not written. Typing is the fallback, and a fallback belongs where the thumb rests, not where the eye lands first.

Uber Eats gives its whole top zone to things that change: a location dropdown, a vertical-switch chip row, a category strip, a filter chip row. Free-text search is a pill inside the bottom tab bar, labelled only "Search", measured 41 pt, and it stays there on every other screen too, so it is chrome rather than content. Airtasker keeps search on the page and makes it grey and secondary, pushed right of the headline.

Buys the whole top zone for content and for the two variables that genuinely move, plus permanent thumb reach instead of a scroll-to-top. Costs discoverability for whoever arrived with a name in their head, a break with platform convention, and most of all it removes the exit, which means the first viewport now has to be good enough to browse. This position is only honest if the feed is genuinely worth reading.

The measured spine. Four control heights from downloaded captures: Airbnb's pill 55 pt, Square Go's field 51 pt, Uber Eats' bottom pill 41 pt, Fresha iOS 0 pt. Every mobile entry control across nineteen iOS home screens is one row or absent. Not one of them shows more than a single row of search chrome.

Fresha iOS has no search control on home at all

And it is right. The app is on the phone of somebody who already installed it, and whose next action is overwhelmingly "the same place again" or "somewhere I already saved", which is exactly what the first two blocks after the greeting are.

Search-first answers "who are you and what do you do". Browse-first answers "welcome back". They are not competing designs, they are answers to different questions, and Fresha ships both because it receives both questions on different doors. The reasoning is about population, not platform.

The honest limit on that reading: the population difference is inferred. Traffic sources were not observed and Mobbin cannot show them. If Fresha's web traffic is mostly returning users then this explanation is wrong and the web hero is simply a legacy landing page. That is the weakest link in this section, and it is also the load-bearing inference in it.

A second disagreement on the same axis: how much to reveal at once

Zocdoc shows one field, then walks you through three separate full screens: a specialty list, a visit-reason list, a location picker. Airbnb shows four fields and one accordion. The total number of decisions is roughly the same. Zocdoc distributes it across screens; Airbnb concentrates it into one object. Zocdoc's version has lower perceived effort and hidden depth. Airbnb's has higher perceived effort and a visible end.

Which is right turns on one thing: can your user abandon? Somebody hunting a dermatologist will finish a three-screen wizard because the need is not optional. Somebody comparing salons on a Tuesday evening will not, and for them a visible end matters more than a gentle start. Neither app is wrong; both are matched to their own abandonment risk, and that will not collapse into a recommendation, because the two products genuinely differ.

Where Solen sits on this question

SearchBar.tsx is position 1 with a desktop geometry and a mobile geometry that has no counterpart in the corpus. Three collapsed segments, one for the service, one for the city and one for the time, plus an ink commit button. On desktop it becomes a 60 px pill capped at 820 px and centred, which is precisely the Airbnb-and-Fresha web geometry, and that half is already right. On mobile the same three segments stack, and HEIGHT.mobile.collapsed is 250 px (SearchBar.tsx:100), roughly 4.5x the nearest mobile comparable, on the screen where space is scarcest.

That is not an argument that 250 is wrong. It is an argument that 250 is a position nobody else holds, so it should be held deliberately or not at all. And Solen already owns the expansion mechanism (SearchOverlay with segment tabs, imported at SearchBar.tsx:30), so collapsing mobile to one row is a geometry change and not a new system. Per the one rule every app here agrees on, it should collapse to the summary form, not the invitation form.

Nothing in this lens argues for a fourth field. The Airbnb Experiences finding and the Fresha finding both say an appointment is a point plus a window, which is exactly what the existing time segment already is. I looked for the argument that we need a fourth and could not find one, which means I may just be agreeing with what ships. Push back if you think date and time should split.

Question two of four

What is the card actually selling?

Squint at a card until only the biggest thing survives. What is left is the currency, and it is a claim about what the user is deciding on. Four answers came back, and they are not four styles, they are four different theories of the decision.

1

The room. The card sells a place you will sit in.

THE BELIEF. You are about to spend forty-five minutes inside this business, in a chair, with a stranger's hands on you. What you are actually screening for is whether it looks like a place you belong. Price and time are logistics you resolve after that.

Fresha's web search card is the complete statement, and it is more interesting than "a salon card": it carries four currencies at once and ranks them by position. The room gets the area, the business gets the title, the price gets a right-aligned column attached to a named service ("Blowout / 30 mins" on the left, "from US$48" on the right), and the hour gets the only colour on the card.

WHAT IT BUYS

A photograph is the fastest possible input. It answers a question the user cannot articulate and would not answer honestly in a filter. It also satisfies an imagery floor for free, because the content is the image.

WHAT IT COSTS

The photo is the largest element, so it spends more card space than anything else, and it only earns that space if it discriminates. Where every venue in a category photographs the same, the biggest element on the card is carrying no information. Square Go's small "Google" attribution chip is a quiet admission that this is real in this category: they source venue photos from Google Places and label them, which is what you do when the photo is a slot to fill rather than the product. Second cost: a room says nothing about who will be holding the scissors, and in this trade that is the thing that goes wrong.

Suits products where the venue is genuinely differentiated and the visit is part of the purchase: spas, restaurants, hotels, studios. Harms products where the practitioner travels to you, and products whose venues are visually interchangeable, because then the card has made its biggest promise with its least informative element.

2

The person. The card sells a human you are hiring.

THE BELIEF. The outcome is inseparable from who performs it, and you cannot evaluate a stranger's skill from a listing, so what you actually do is a risk assessment on a face. Give the user the face at the largest size the layout allows and let them do the thing they were going to do anyway.

Care.com is the cleanest case: a portrait photograph in the card's photo slot, "Bria G." as the title, then an hourly from-price and an experience line in grey, then a green-check line reading "Hired by 2 neighbors". Note the name form, a first name plus a last initial: identity is partial on purpose. Bloom labels its practitioners by temperament, "Direct & Empowering" and "Empathetic & Caring", rather than by credentials. For a service where you sit in a chair being talked to for an hour, that is a more honest currency than years of experience, and it is the only instance of it anywhere in the corpus.

AND TWO BEAUTY COUNTER-SIGNALS THAT CUT AGAINST IT. Careem's staff step pre-selects "Auto assign / We'll assign the best professional", leftmost, over two named, photographed, rated staff. And Fresha's own Team tab ships initial monograms, A and TK and EL, with no photographs at all, and the load-bearing text is the ROLE ("Junior lash artist", "Esthetician"), not the face. Two of the five beauty products observable anywhere are saying the face is optional and the role is not.

Buys the one input a listing cannot fake and the user genuinely wants, and it changes the emotional register of the whole app from a directory of shops to a marketplace of people. Costs photography you do not control and may not have, and Fresha's monograms are the evidence of the failure mode: if faces are the anatomy and the faces are missing, the card degrades to a coloured circle with a letter in it, which is a worse card than a room photo. It also invites appearance-based sorting, which in a beauty market is a real fairness exposure rather than a hypothetical. Harms multi-chair salons with staff turnover, because the card promises a person the business cannot guarantee will be there.

3

The work. The card sells an outcome, and the person is a credit line.

THE BELIEF. You cannot evaluate a stranger, but you can evaluate their output. A photograph of a finished result is a checkable claim; a photograph of a face is not. So show the result and put the name underneath in small type.

Airtasker's beauty row photographs a finished eyebrow. Redfin's card photo is a house the agent sold, badged with the sale price: the price shown is the work's price, not the fee. Behance makes the structure literal, where a strip of four work thumbnails IS the card and the person's avatar is a small badge overlapping its bottom edge.

Airbnb Services is the extreme, and the most relevant screen in the sweep. Every card's photo is a still life of a tool or material: a gua sha stone on cloth, a dry body brush, a candle beside a loofah. Not a face, not a room. And every title reads "Balinese Soul Purification by Putu", "Chakra balancing and sound healing by Ayu": the practitioner's first name is grammatically inside the product title. The company whose entire design language was built on selling a place changed the subject of the photograph the moment it started selling a service, and it did the trust work with a first name.

Buys the only currency that is actually about quality, and the only one that scales emotionally: a wall of good haircuts is aspirational in a way that a wall of shopfronts is not. Costs result photography, per salon, continuously, which is a content-operations commitment and not a design decision, and it is why most booking products do not do this. Second cost, structural: result photos are not comparable. Two beautiful photographs tell you nothing about which is cheaper, nearer or sooner, so a work-first feed is a browsing surface that cannot also be a deciding surface. Harms honesty too: the photo is a best case selected by the seller, and unlike a price or a slot there is no way to constrain it.

4

The slot. The card sells an hour.

THE BELIEF. The user already decided roughly where and roughly who. What they are fighting is their own calendar. The scarce good is not the service, it is 14:30 on Thursday, and whoever surfaces that first wins the booking.

Tock demotes the venue photo to a small thumbnail so the card body can hold slot groups under labelled sub-headers. Resy fills its slot pills blue and lets the price be a band of dollar signs, so the hour is the only colour on the card. TheFork hangs a green "-30%" chip under each individual time, so the discount reads as a property of the hour.

And two apps render a DATE rather than a clock time, which matters here specifically. Zocdoc's result rows end in a full-width yellow button reading "Next available: Mon, Jun 8", by a distance the loudest thing on the row. And Square Go's search rows carry no venue photo at all: the business name, a prose list of services, then a rating, then "Wed, August 30", then a distance. Both of the closest analogues to us arrived at a date rather than a clock time on their own.

Buys a collapsed funnel: a card that shows an hour is a card you can commit from, so a browse surface becomes a booking surface without adding a screen. Costs three real things. Space, because slots are text and a row of chips takes the vertical room a photo would have used. An honesty liability, because a slot on a browse card is a promise about live inventory and a stale one is discovered at the worst possible moment. And it forecloses browsing, because a screen that asks "which hour" has already assumed you know what you want. Suits the haircut you get every six weeks. Harms first-time high-deliberation purchases, and any product without reliable real-time availability.

5

The price. Named as the foil, not as an option.

This is not one of the four, because in every observed instance an app makes price the largest element only when its sellers are substitutable, and no local salon market is. The evidence is unusually clean: Preply sets "$18" larger than the tutor's name, lists 3,368 tutors, and defaults its sort to "Price: lowest first". Three thousand interchangeable sellers of a commodity hour, sorted by price, because price is the only axis that discriminates. World of Hyatt turns the price into a filled blue block overlapping the photo, so the price literally is the button.

This is the position Solen must not drift into, and naming it is the whole answer to whether a legally forced price is a burden.

Eight ways the apps that DO show a price keep it from shouting

This is the operationally useful part, because Solen has no choice about presence and total choice about volume. Every one of these was observed.

  • Right-align it into a column and attach it to a named service, so it reads as a table cell rather than an offer. Shangri-La's spa goes all the way to a literal two-column table with the headers "Duration" and "Price". A luxury spa deciding that price is data.
  • Put it last in the row. Fresha's iOS service list orders name, duration, description, and only then "from $40" in small grey.
  • Give it peers. Alan's confirmation screen is the best instance: three icon rows of identical size and weight, an avatar row for the psychologist, a calendar row for the time, and a banknote row for the fee. A number in a row of numbers stops being an offer and becomes a specification.
  • Shrink the unit, not the number. IHG's compound numeral: a big "327", a small "USD / night".
  • Demote it below the CTA. Alan's practitioner profile puts a full-width button and then, underneath it, small and grey, the from-price and the free-cancellation window.
  • Make it a band, not a point. Skillshare's "$20 - $85". A band refuses to imply a precision the data does not have.
  • Prefix it. "from", "From", "Starting at". Universal across Fresha, Square Go, Airbnb Services, Care.com, Airtasker and Peerspace.
  • Give the loud treatment to something else. The highest-leverage one and the least obvious. On the Fresha search card the price is not small, it is quiet by comparison, because the time chips are the only coloured elements on the card. Loudness is relative. The cheapest way to stop a price shouting is to hand one other element the colour.

A haircut is genuinely both, and this is the crux. The face is a real risk instrument (they will touch you, and a bad result is visible on your body for six weeks) and the work is a real quality instrument (a photo of their fade tells you more about their fade than their face does).

Where Solen sits on this question

Solen takes position 1: the photo is the largest element and its subject is the venue. And the price is already at the quietest setting the type system allows, read off SalonCard.tsx on 2026-07-30: 12 px, greyest token, lightest weight, last row, right-aligned, sharing a line with the address, with the word "from" gated behind a named service because Art. 13 of the price-disclosure ordinance requires a minimum price to describe the concrete offer it buys.

That law is an advantage, and the corpus is what makes that arguable rather than consoling. Every serious booking product ends up showing a price anyway: Fresha ships a per-service from-price on its search card on both platforms, plus a Maximum price filter. And what the law forced us to build is the expensive part, a per-service minimum with the service name attached, which is a data-model commitment and is exactly what Fresha's search card is. We were made to build it in year one.

What we give up by taking that view, stated so it is not smuggled: the option of a genuinely calm inspiration rail. Fresha's home card is calmer than ours because it carries a category chip where ours carries a price, and calm is not nothing. If the home rails should ever read as pure inspiration, the price is the element in the way, and that would be a legal question about whether a rail counts as a price-disclosure context, not a taste one.

The one thing that would change this whole answer, and nobody has measured it: whether Solen's venue photographs actually discriminate between venues. A photo earns the largest slot by discriminating. Airbnb's rooms discriminate enormously; a treehouse is not a loft. If a Basel Coiffeur interior and the Coiffeur two streets away photograph nearly identically, then we have spent the biggest element on the card on something that does not move the decision. That needs a look at the real seeded photo set and a between-venue variance number.

Question three of four

What does home owe somebody who already knows what they want?

Four positions, and they are genuinely different bets about what "return" is. One product often runs two of them at once, which is itself a finding.

A

Pin the commitment on home

THE BELIEF. A user with an outstanding obligation is usually opening the app about that obligation, so home's job is to close that loop before it offers anything new.

Zocdoc is the fullest version: "Hi Alex", a search field, a chip strip, then "Up next" with the date and time as the boldest text on the screen, the doctor's name, the reason, an embedded map tile with the doctor's face as the map pin, and a three-icon action row for phone, calendar and directions. The first browse section is cut off at the fold. Starbucks' "Your Usuals", a row of three circular drink photos each with a "+" baked into the image, is that company's entire returning-user proposition.

Buys zero navigation to the thing they would otherwise hunt for, plus somewhere to hang the actions that only exist while a commitment is live: directions, phone, calendar, cancel. Costs the largest single block of first-viewport space. On the Zocdoc screen a returning user sees no browse content at all above the fold, and the same screen shows a first-timer a gap where a card they cannot have would go. Suits imminent, actionable commitments: a delivery arriving in 25 minutes, a dermatologist tomorrow. Harms anything weeks out. A haircut on the 14th of next month does not need a map on your home screen today, and pinning it spends the best slot on the page for 34 of 35 days to answer a question nobody asked.

B

Give return its own destination, keep home for browse

THE BELIEF. Return is a mode, not a row, and a mode deserves an address. Home is the browse instrument and stays identical for everybody. ClassPass is the cleanest holder: its home opens on "Browse by credits" tiles and mentions the reservation nowhere.

And this is where the cost is measurable in cash. Angi built a full-screen illustrated explainer whose only job is to teach people that its "Pros" tab exists: "We know how valuable it is to find a pro you trust. So we've made it easier than ever to work with them again." Nobody builds an onboarding page for something users find on their own. That screen is the invoice for this position.

Buys unlimited room for the personal content, one home screen to design and grade instead of two, and a count badge that does the reminding almost free. Costs a tap on the single highest-intent action in the product, plus a discovery problem, plus the dead-end empty destination: of sixteen empty saved-list screens read, fourteen are an illustration, a headline, a subline and a large blank. Suits products where browse is the recurring job every session. Harms products where the personal action IS the dominant action.

C

Put return inside the search instrument

THE BELIEF. Return is not a separate desire, it is a shortcut through the same funnel, so it belongs where intent is already declared. Uber puts two recent destinations directly under "Where to?" as plain text rows with a clock icon, no photo, no card, no shadow, costing roughly an eighth of the viewport for two entries against Zocdoc's third for one, and "Suggestions" still makes the fold.

The most sophisticated version is Fiverr, whose "Pick up where you left off" has a switcher between keep-exploring, saved services, and the user's literal last query. Return modelled as three different memories of the same user, with the user picking which one they are today.

Buys almost no home real estate, and it costs a first-timer literally nothing, because an empty dropdown is invisible rather than sad. Costs invisibility in the other direction too: it does no reminding and builds no habit, since nothing about a dropdown teaches a user that the app remembers them. And it cannot carry a commitment: a date, a time and a cancellation window do not belong in a search suggestion. Suits a repeat destination. Harms anything with a live obligation.

D

One screen, section present or absent

THE BELIEF. Personalisation is a section-level property, never a screen-level one. There is one home; it grows a band when there is history and drops it when there is not.

The proof is a clean natural experiment. Expedia's home signed in with recents and the same page with no recents row: identical search box, identical browse grid directly below, one section present in one and absent in the other, and the shorter page does not read as broken, because the browse content closes the gap. Same at Starbucks: the Gold member's home against the new member's home with "Your Usuals" simply gone.

A SIDE FINDING WORTH PULLING OUT. A third Expedia capture shows "Sign in" in the header AND a recent-searches row. So the trigger is history, not authentication. You do not need accounts to have a returning-user surface.

Costs the sparse-but-present state, which is the worst of the three: Vrbo's recently-viewed row holds one card and leaves an obvious hole to its right, and HBO Max's "My List" holds two posters on a row sized for seven. Absence is invisible; sparseness reads as a bug. Second cost: the personal content competes with browse on equal visual terms, so a row that looks like every other row does not announce that it is yours, which is precisely the reminding job position A does well and this one gives up.

The distinction I would defend hardest: Airbnb's returning user returns to a CATEGORY, and Solen's returns to a PERSON. Airbnb's whole return architecture admits it. Question four of four

How many things should the first screen show?

The answer this sweep produced was not the expected one. Density is not a property of a product, it is a property of the decision a single screen is asking for. The same company ships a sparse screen and a dense screen days apart, on purpose, and both are right.

1

The catalogue index. You know what you want, I just give you the word for it.

THE BELIEF. The catalogue is far too large to browse and the user's intent is already formed, so the first screen is a table of contents and the unit of density is nameable categories, not products. Blinkit fits twelve grocery categories plus a cropped thirteenth row in one phone viewport, Fresha fourteen, and Meituan goes past thirty tappable targets in a single viewport.

Buys two-tap reach into a catalogue of any size, and because positions are stable, repeat users navigate by muscle memory and stop reading the labels at all. Costs all persuasion: no desire, no browsing pleasure, no sense of what is good. It also goes rigid, because once users navigate by position, re-ordering the grid is a breaking change. And it needs volume to be honest: Time2book runs this grammar with two service rows and roughly 40% of the viewport empty, and it reads as a stub rather than a product.

2

The comparison table. The kind is settled, one field decides which.

THE BELIEF. The user has finished deciding what and is resolving which, and resolution happens by scanning one comparable field down a column. Every pixel that is not that field is friction. Booking.com's results row carries fourteen to sixteen discrete elements. ClassPass is the purest case: seven classes per viewport with zero photography, each row being time, duration, class name, instructor and a spots-left number.

THE MECHANISM, STATED ONCE BECAUSE IT RECURS EVERYWHERE. In every member of this position, the photograph shrinks or vanishes in exact proportion to how many bookable times the row carries. And ClassPass brings the photo back exactly once, full-width, on the class detail page, at the moment of commitment. That is a theory of what a photo is for: it answers "what will this room feel like", and that question only becomes live once the time is settled.

Buys fast confident resolution, and confidence rises with the number of rows visible at once, because a choice made from eight visible options feels checked and a choice from two feels guessed. Costs the photograph, so it can satisfy desire but never create it. And it commoditises the supply side: when eight providers render as eight rows differing only in a number, the user optimises the number, which is exactly what a provider does not want. Harms anything chosen on feel, and any seller whose margin depends on not being comparable.

3

The single-object showcase. You cannot be told what this is, you have to see it.

THE BELIEF. The product is differentiated and not describable in fields. One unit per viewport, the photo is most of the unit, the text is two lines. Airbnb iOS shows about 1.9 cards; Resy's Discover feed about 1.2 units.

Resy is the honest test of this position, because Resy does not hold it consistently. Its editorial index drops the same content to seven rows per viewport with the photo demoted to a small right-hand square. One hero to set the mood, then a table to make the choice. That is not inconsistency, it is sequencing.

Buys desire, brand, and permission to charge more. It is the only position that can make an expensive infrequent purchase feel considered rather than transacted. Costs a viewport of scrolling per item, so a large catalogue becomes unreachable by browsing and search has to carry everything. And it hides the deciding field, which does not remove the decision, it moves it one screen deeper. Harms any product under a price-display obligation, because a two-line body has no room for a price plus the thing the price is for.

4

The intent router. Anything I show you before I know where and when you are is noise.

THE BELIEF. For location-and-time-bound inventory, no pre-filter list can be relevant, so the honest first screen shows no inventory at all and asks the one question that makes inventory meaningful. Booking.com's home genuinely surprised me: the most notoriously dense travel product in the world opens with a hero, a headline, a four-field bar, two promo banners and two recent-search tiles. Zero properties in the first viewport. Swiggy is the most extreme: three mode tiles and roughly 40% of the viewport deliberately empty, shipped by one of the densest apps in India.

AND ONE DETAIL THAT SAVES THIS POSITION FROM BEING PURELY AESTHETIC. Fresha's hero carries a live number. Six separate captures of the same screen read 217,913 then 320,602 then 320,692 then 320,797 then 320,898 then 456,602 appointments booked today (one, two, the sixth). It is demonstrably wired, not a decorative string. That counter is the one thing on an empty hero that does the job this position otherwise cannot: it proves supply exists without listing any of it.

Buys one unambiguous action, zero wrong-city inventory, a clean funnel, and it can never go stale or look thin. Costs teaching the visitor nothing about supply: a first-timer cannot tell whether you have 12 salons or 1,200, and for a marketplace that has not yet earned trust that is the single most important thing the home page could say. Harms pre-launch marketplaces with no brand, and repeat users, for whom the question is already answered and the screen is a toll booth.

The part no document in this estate had

Which of these choices depend on which others

Every floor in our own design law scopes itself to one screen. A repo-wide search for a rule binding one choice to another returns nothing. So here are the edges. Three kinds, and they are not degrees of the same thing.

FORCED

Choosing X makes Y near-mandatory. You still choose how to pay. You do not choose whether.

F1

If a bookable time goes on the card, then either the photo shrinks or the card count halves. There is no third source.

A slot is a tap target, so it cannot be shorter than about 44 pt, and the text rows are already at their floor, so the height comes out of the only elastic element on the card, which is the photograph. Four products pay the same bill in four different currencies on the same data: Tock pays with the photo (a thumbnail, two labelled slot groups, two venues fit), Resy pays with the count (full photo, pills run off the right edge, about two cards), TheFork overpays both at roughly 1.2 cards, and Zocdoc pays the maximum at exactly one row per viewport, its day tiles carrying a count of appointments each.

For us: our imagery lock makes the photo the largest element of every card, so Tock's payment method is closed, which means a time forces Resy's, fewer cards. And we are already at four columns at the medium breakpoint against our own floor of six. This is why the next-free-day direction and the density floor cannot both be satisfied. Neither lens said that on its own, because each was looking at one axis.

F2

If the photo gets bigger, then there are fewer columns, then the name truncates, then the name quietly stops being a field you can compare on.

Photo area grows with the square of column width; text does not shrink to compensate, it clips. Etsy at four across truncates every single title mid-phrase; Faire holds five only because its body is two short lines. Columns and fields-per-card are one decision, taken twice.

The consequence nobody wrote down: closing our desktop rail from four cards to the six our own floor asks for makes every long Swiss salon name truncate harder. The worst-case-content gate has to run at the new column count before the count changes, not after.

F3

If a face goes in the photo slot, then density must drop, because a face below thumbnail size stops being a face and silently becomes a row identifier.

A photograph of a room is read as information and survives being small. A photograph of a person is read as a person, and that reading needs resolution. Shrink it far enough and you are paying for portrait photography and getting an identifier. Care.com holds about two rows per viewport; Preply about two cards.

So "the person, not the premises" is a three-floor change wearing the costume of a one-field change: density, then the desktop column count, then imagery.

F4

If a section title claims a judgment, then it needs a byline, a count or a date, because a judgment with no owner and no timestamp is unfalsifiable.

Nine products, all of them, and all of them also rotate the photograph to a small left square the moment prose enters, because prose is a horizontal medium. Medium: "List curated by Medium Staff". Swarm carries an author avatar, "Updated August 2025", and "3 places", all three at once. The Infatuation adds a written line and a date, plus "SORTED BY MOST RECENT" as a header, which is the editorial equivalent of showing your working.

The cheap half and the expensive half is the operationally useful part. A count and a date are derivable from data at zero content cost. Prose and a named curator are a content-operations commitment. So this does not force us into publishing, it forces a choice between the derivable tier and dropping the editorial claim. "Salon of the Month" is a superlative with no stated basis, which makes it the section where the cheap tier would do the most work.

F5

If category tiles carry real supply counts, then the index stops being navigation and becomes proof of supply, and you are committed to printing the thin numbers.

A count is only evidence if it is printed when it is small. An index that shows counts only where they flatter is a billboard, and users detect that fast, because the categories with no number are exactly the ones they wanted. Every app doing this prints the thin case: Whatnot's "6 Options", Unity's "Templates (1)", Alma printing a literal "0", and Airbnb Services rendering seven "Coming soon" tiles rather than hiding the categories it has not launched. The cheapest version of all is Preply putting the count in its headline, "100+ Japanese tutors".

This is the cheapest honest supply proof available to a pre-launch marketplace, and it needs no invented number. The forced consequence is that it publishes how thin each Swiss category currently is. That is a business decision about disclosure, not a design decision.

F6

If the entry control displays state, then it needs an in-place edit path AND a second control beside it.

Once the control shows the current query it becomes the only legible place to change it, so without an edit path the state is a dead end. And the fields that make a result exist (what, where, when) are a different set from the fields that shrink a result set (price, sort, venue type). One control cannot be both without becoming a form. Fresha iOS is the complete case in one screen: a summary pill, then a chip row of "Sort by", "Max price" and "Venue type", then "21 venues nearby", then the cards. Three separate instruments. Care.com asks the money question twice, once in a five-segment bar and once as a rail slider: the bar decides what exists, the rail decides what survives.

This is the bill for collapsing our mobile control to the summary form. We already own the edit path. What we do not have is a refiner row on home, and that is not an argument to add one, it is the reason the summary form is not the free option it looks like.

INCOMPATIBLE

X and Y want the same scarce resource and there is not enough for both. One wins, or both get degraded into something neither wanted.

I1

A pinned personal block and a photo-dominant first screen cannot both own the top. But the resolution the corpus actually uses is not "pick one", it is to strip the personal block's photograph.

Marriott makes "CONTINUE YOUR BOOKING" a text-only row with a chevron and no image at all, and recently-viewed a small left thumbnail. Fresha demotes "Book again" to a thumbnail row sitting above photo-top Favourites. And World of Hyatt is the control case: no personal block at all, and its photographic cards land immediately.

Our imagery floor is an AREA rule, so a text or thumbnail personal row costs it almost nothing, because it was never going to be a photograph. The incompatibility is between a personal block and a photo-DOMINANT block, not between a personal block and the imagery floor. That is a fourth option both of the individual lenses missed. Its cost, stated so it is not smuggled: it is exactly the second-anatomy-for-one-entity problem, which lands on I5.

I2

A category grid above the fold and real inventory in the first viewport cannot both fit on a phone. The choice is not grid or inventory, it is which one is first.

Expedia, Trip.com and Rappi all resolve it against inventory. But Fresha's fourteen-tile grid, the largest in the corpus, is the FOURTH section on its screen, after the greeting, Book again, Favourites and Trending. It is not deleted, it is demoted below the fold. The app with the biggest grid puts it fourth. We render ours first.

I3

The bottom edge holds exactly one persistent thing.

Of ten screens read, five drop the tab bar to hold a commit CTA (BlaBlaCar, Careem, with the price inside the button, KakaoTalk), three keep the tab bar and move the action INTO the card (Peerspace, StubHub), and one keeps both only by making the bottom row a non-commit utility row (komoot).

Uber Eats can put search in the bottom bar because its home has no commit action. Our PDP has a sticky book bar. So moving search to the bottom on home would make the bottom edge mean "search" on one surface and "commit" on the next, which is our consistency law applied to chrome rather than to cards. That is a cost the entry lens did not price, because the PDP was outside it.

I4

Three currencies do not fit one rail card. This is the strongest evidence on the page, because the evidence is a search that failed.

I searched specifically for a provider card carrying a photograph, a from-price and bookable times as three peer elements. Ten screens came back and not one of them does it. Zocdoc: eight time pills and no price anywhere. Alan: the price demoted under the CTA and a day WORD rather than a slot. Square Go splits them across levels, the time as a sublabel inside the Book button and the price on the service row.

The one product that carries all three, Fresha's search card, manages it by ceasing to be a card and becoming a service table at one unit per viewport.

So the real menu is narrower than either lens implied. Our current configuration, a price with no time, was observed in no app. And the configuration we would move to, photo plus price plus time at rail density, is also observed in no app, and this is why. The four honest options are: drop the price on the rail (legally fraught), drop the time (the status quo), split across levels like Square Go, or accept one unit per viewport, which is a search-page density and not a home-rail density.

I5

One entity cannot serve four jobs through one component, and "documented variant" stops being true somewhere before four.

ClassPass renders the same studio four different ways: a photographic banner running the whole width, with the name set in white on the photo, a circular avatar in a horizontal strip, a plain text row with no imagery at all, and a photo-top card on home. Each one is right for its job. But the photograph moves from a full-width banner to a circle to absent, and the name moves from on-photo to under-photo to mid-row. No single component produces that range by prop.

So the rebook-rail direction's first question is not "should home rebook". It is "how many anatomies is SalonCard allowed to have", and that has to be answered before the row is designed rather than discovered afterwards.

I6

A fixed-width personal rail and a two-item history cannot both look intentional.

Fresha web ships "Favourites" holding exactly one card in a row sized for four, with three empty columns beside it. Preply renders one favourite tutor and then an entirely grey screen.

The corpus contains exactly one fix: ClassPass ends its saved-places strip with a dashed-outline circular "+", so a three-item strip is never short. It fills the hole with an affordance instead of with content, which costs nothing and fabricates nothing.

ONLY-TOGETHER

X is not wrong without Y. It is inert without Y. It occupies space and returns nothing.

T1

An inventory-free first viewport is inert without a number that carries its own provenance.

A hero-only viewport makes a claim and gives the visitor no way to check it. A number can substitute for the missing inventory, but only a checkable one, because an unverifiable number is read as copy and copy is exactly what a visitor discounts. Four provenance devices, all observed: unrounded precision (Whop's "$2,857,094,832", digits nobody writes by hand), a freshness stamp (Zillow's "Updated on 4/30/2026"), a named source (Kraken asterisking its claim to Forbes Advisor), and a stated window and denominator (Employment Hero's "Based on 199 Service Ratings over the past year").

The control case is the useful one. Babbel prints "65% of our users are beginners!" with no precision, no source, no window and no denominator. It reads as marketing, and it is the exact shape our own stat rule bans.

Pre-launch, we have no provenance-carrying number, so the inventory-free first viewport is unavailable to us as a matter of a missing precondition, not as a matter of taste. The two affordable substitutes are F5's per-category real counts, or Preply's move of putting the count in the headline. Honest negative: a search for a live counter on a marketplace product home returned ten SaaS and marketing landing pages and zero marketplace product surfaces, so Fresha remains a sample of one in its own category, and the rest of the evidence for this pattern comes from a genre with a different honesty standard.

T2

A segmented bar is inert unless every segment is a real, wired constraint on the result set.

A segment is a promise that the field changes the answer. A field that does not filter costs a tap and teaches a false model of what the product indexes, which is worse than costing a tap. Fresha's four segments each map to something wired, and the field it does not have, price, appears instead as a "Max price" refiner on the search surface.

This is our own silent-no-op law arriving from the design side. The arity of the entry control is a claim about the backend, so "how many segments" cannot be decided separately from "which of them actually filters". Our three all discriminate today. The force is prospective: a fourth segment needs the discriminate proof before the design, not after. The entry lens looked for an argument for a fourth field and found none, and this is why that absence is a good thing rather than a gap.

T3

An editorial title is inert without curation, and the affordable version of curation is to state the selection mechanism rather than claim a curator.

Three tiers. Expensive: a named curator plus prose. Cheap: attribution of the mechanism, which is derivable, as in Vrbo's "Because you viewed vacation homes in Bangkok" or Etsy's "Your recommendations get better as you favorite more things". Free: a count or a date. "Salon of the Month" makes the strongest editorial claim on our home screen with the least support, so it is the one section where the cheap tier would do real work.

T4

A personal rail needs three things together: a live read, a backfill, and a minimum count. Drop any one and it produces a named, observed failure.

No live read: nothing renders, and fabricating one is banned outright. No backfill: the dead-end empty state, which is fourteen of sixteen empty saved-list screens in the corpus. No minimum count: the sparse rail, which is Fresha web's one-card Favourites and Vrbo's hole.

Our own RecentlyViewed has the live read and the backfill and not the minimum count: one entry renders the rail. The return lens went looking for a defect in that component, said it could not find one and might just be agreeing. The defect is the third leg, and it is visible only from the relations view, because the count rule belongs to a different lens than the component does.

T5

A minimal or wordless entry control is inert unless the location it silently resolved is asserted somewhere checkable.

If the screen never asks where you are, you cannot know that it knows, so "Nearby" is an unverifiable claim and the user has to take the ranking on faith. Square Go's wordless field is paired with a section title reading "Recommended in 94025". Fresha iOS does both at once: the pill ends in a truncated "Current location" and the list is headed "21 venues nearby".

Our Nearby section already receives a count prop and a city bar already exists, so the assertion slot is built. The relation says that if the mobile control ever shrinks, that assertion stops being a nicety and becomes the thing that makes the shrink honest.

If you only decide one thing

The choice that decides the most

What the card's largest element is a picture of, and which fact is second-loudest. The currency.

The argument is edge count across layers, not importance. Fix the currency and the following eight things stop being open questions:

  • Who supplies the photography. Venue shots, staff portraits, or continuous result photography. That is a content-operations commitment, not a design decision.
  • Cards per viewport, then cards per row on desktop, then whether the name truncates (F2), then whether the name is still a field you can compare on.
  • Whether a real availability read is a prerequisite, and whether the rail is a commit surface or a read surface (F1).
  • The entity. Venue or practitioner, which fixes the data model, what a rebook row names, and what the URL is about.
  • Whether the surface can decide or only browse, which fixes how much work the entry control has to do, which fixes its arity (T2) and therefore its geometry.
  • What must be proven elsewhere, because if the card cannot demonstrate supply then the category index or the headline has to (F5, T1).
  • How many anatomies one component must produce (I5), which is what makes or breaks the same-thing-looks-the-same-everywhere law.
  • Whether three currencies are attempted at all, which I4 already answers: they do not fit at rail density, so the currency decision is also the decision about what the rail is for.

Why the runner-up loses, stated fairly, because it is genuinely close

The rival is must the first viewport contain real inventory. It decides the entry control's geometry, the hero's height, whether a personal band can pin above the fold, whether the category grid can be first, and the density number. Five edges, which is respectable.

It loses because all five are layout edges, and layout is the cheapest thing in this estate to change. Hero.tsx proves it: roughly 120 lines of comment over about 40 lines of JSX, documenting six separate revisions of the same vertical rhythm, each one reversible in an afternoon. The currency decision commits photography, a data model, a backend availability read, where a statutory price can sit quietly, and the component graph. Those are not afternoons.

The condition that would flip this, named so it is falsifiable

If Solen's venue photographs do not discriminate between venues, the photograph is not carrying the decision, most of the currency question collapses, and the first-viewport question becomes the most load-bearing choice by default. So the single measurement that decides which question matters most is the between-venue photo variance measurement on the real seeded set, and nobody has run it. It is worth more than any further sweep.

The sharpest single edge, and why one settled decision was bigger than it looked

The most consequential sub-decision inside the currency is whether a bookable time appears on the card. That row was deleted on 2026-07-13 on card-composition grounds, via an owner-approved mockup, and by the precedence chain that call stands. What F1 and I4 add is that the same decision also settled the photo-versus-count question and bounded the desktop column count, because a card with a time cannot hold six across with a 5:4 photo. It was a currency decision wearing the clothes of a composition decision. That is not an argument to reverse it. It is an argument that if it is ever revisited, the revisit is three floors wide, not one row wide.

What could not be determined

  • Motion, gesture and transition, entirely. Mobbin returns stills. Nothing about timing, easing, how a pill expands into an accordion, or whether a web bar collapses on scroll. One capture strongly suggests a scroll-collapse; the end state was seen and the trigger was not, so it is not claimed.
  • Whether any of this converts. Zero engagement, retention or conversion data anywhere in the sample. Nothing here should be cited as evidence that a pattern moves a number.
  • Whether any of these apps tested the choice. Every belief is reconstructed from the artefact, so a deliberate bet and an accident that shipped are indistinguishable from here.
  • The pure beauty-booking comparison set is two apps. Booksy and Treatwell would change these documents if they were observable, and they are not. Any future claim about them needs a live capture, not this work.
  • Whether a stylist's face on a card increases bookings. No behavioural data anywhere in the corpus.
  • Whether Solen's venue photos discriminate between venues. The strongest unverified claim in the whole body of work, and the measurement that decides which question above is the most load-bearing.
  • Solen's rendered geometry. Every Solen number here was read from source on 2026-07-30 with the line cited. Nothing was rendered. One screenshot at 390x844 and one at 1440x900 would settle where the fold actually lands, and that is the first step before acting on any of it.
  • Where the density switch sits numerically. The variable is nameable (one comparable field versus accumulated feel) and the threshold is not. Nobody in the sample published one, and inventing a number here would repeat a mistake this system has already had to disown once.

Two stale numbers this work corrected rather than inherited

md:min-h-[92dvh] is not in Hero.tsx. It appears four times and every one is inside a comment block, one of which records its own removal. Three documents assert it as live. And HEIGHT.mobile.collapsed is 250, not 280; the 280 appears twice in the comment history above the constant, which is where it was picked up. The direction of that argument survives at roughly 4.5x instead of 5x. The number does not.

Shared root cause, because it is the same failure twice and it will happen again. Both values were read out of comment blocks in files that carry their entire tuning history inline. The live value and six retired values sit in one visual block, and a grep returns all seven. The cheap mitigation for any future sweep: read the constant or the className, never the block above it.
