Direction E. The query, not the catalogue. Home's job is to get three answers out of you (what, where, when) before it shows you anything, because a salon list built on a guess is a promise we then have to take back. The screen is a form, then the answer to that form. Read top to bottom it is one sentence: you said Coiffeur, in Zurich, on Saturday, so here are the 24 salons that can actually take you.

What it gives up. It re-asks a returning customer three questions it already knows the answers to; that cost is permanent and unfixable inside this bet. It makes home a poor browsing surface by design, so all merchandising happens after a search. It deletes the mobile category grid from home (MobileCategoriesRow, currently the first thing under the hero at page.tsx:200) and folds the taxonomy into the Service field, which is exactly Fresha web's mechanism and exactly its named cost: somebody who does not know the word Barbershop now has to open a field to find out barbershops exist. It drops the category tag from every result, because the Service field already declared the category and a tag repeating it is meta that adds no decision (copy economy rule 4). It cuts the save-heart and the account button, which serve browsing and account, not this screen's job. And it cannot go all the way to Fresha's version: Fresha's inventory-free hero is legible only because a wired counter sits under it, and pre-launch Solen has no number it can print honestly, so the form shares the first viewport with real salons instead of owning it.

What it does NOT give up, and what paid for that. The direction was written expecting to sacrifice the first salon photo above the fold permanently. It does not have to. Two cuts buy it back: the h1 shrinks to one line, and City and When pair side by side on one row instead of stacking, on Trip.com's from/to row and Skyscanner's two-date row. The form measures 200 px for three fields where SearchBar.tsx:100 spends 250, so the first salon is complete at 703 px and the second one's photo starts at 715 and is cropped at the 844 fold with 129 px of it showing, which is the scroll promise. The date is the one answer the results cannot show, which is why the quiet count line restates only the date and not the whole tuple.

The bet

Home's job is to prove, in the first screen, that there are enough real salons near you to make this worth using. So the content is actual salon cards plus honest per-category counts, and the search control shrinks to one row. It is the direct inverse of direction E: E says show nothing until the query is legal, F says the screen is worthless until it shows real supply.

What it gives up

  • The calm, and the single unambiguous action. The shipped mobile search control is 250px of three stacked segments plus an ink button (SearchBar.tsx:100). This direction cuts it to a 44px row. Anyone who arrives knowing exactly what they want is now served slightly worse so that everyone who does not is served much better.
  • Editorial and inspiration. Salon-of-the-month, the Inspo band and reviews all move below everything on this page. There is no room above the fold and F does not pretend there is.
  • Disclosure control. Real counts mean home publishes how few salons each Swiss category has. This page prints its smallest category and its smallest city in the first screen rather than hiding them. That is the forced consequence of the device and it is a business decision about disclosure, not a design decision, so it is yours (WHY_RELATIONS F5).
  • Name comparability at the edges. Two columns costs 173px of card width, so the longest Swiss compound name truncates (card 6). Per WHY_RELATIONS F2, raising the desktop rail from four cards to the six our own density floor asks for makes this worse, so FLOORS LAW 1(f) has to be re-run at the new column count before the count changes, not after.
  • The card's shipped row order. At 173px, a name and a rating on one baseline leaves the name about 16 characters, so the rating moved down a row and the named from-price took a row of its own. Measured reason, named in the file header, not a preference.

The lens finding behind it

Three things, and the second is the one I would defend hardest.

First, the count device is real and it always prints the thin case. Looked at as images this session, not recalled: Airbnb Services iOS puts the city in its biggest text ("Services in New York City"), then a rail of category tiles each carrying a label and a smaller grey supply line under it, and the smallest of those lines reads "1 available" rather than being suppressed. Wolt uses the identical anatomy on a photo tile ("Burger" then a grey places count under it) and also prints its smallest numbers. Two further devices show up in the same sweep, and both are query-responsive rather than decorative: Fresha's search puts a venue count on its own grey row between the query and the cards, and the same screen with a broader query shows a larger one, so the number is measuring something; Swiggy puts it in the section header instead, and DoorDash gives it a row of its own beside a Reset control. And OpenTable's web home is the shape this page borrows: a hero about a sixth of the viewport high, then immediately five restaurant cards and a cropped sixth, with a demand line printed on the thin rows as well as the busy ones, and grey no-photo placeholders left in place rather than the venue being hidden.

Second, and this is the argument I did not expect to find: F is the only direction that pays the imagery floor the way the owner said it must be paid. The floor asks for roughly a third of the viewport to be photographic, and the 2026-07-25 rejection says in the owner's own words that it is met by showing off the stores we have and never by an image baked into a layout. Every other way of hitting a third of the viewport with photography involves making something bigger. F hits it by making the count higher, so the photographic area on this page is entirely salons' own cover shots. Direction F does not merely comply with that floor. It is what that floor was asking for.

Third, the provenance device this page adds, which is not copied from anything above: the four category counts sum to the anchor. WHY_RELATIONS T1 lists four ways a number earns belief (unrounded precision, a freshness stamp, a named source, a stated window), and all four ask the reader to trust something off-screen. A breakdown that adds up to its own total can be audited without leaving the screen, which is strictly better, and it is why the two non-category tiles the shipped component carries (Karte, Walk-in) are dropped here: a walk-in count would double-count barbershops and break the sum.

One correction to my own reasoning, since it changes the price of this direction

I first wrote that the supply count was a net-new backend dependency, on the strength of npm run exists "supply count" returning nothing. Then I opened the endpoint. It already selects with an exact count and already returns a total beside its items (app/api/salons/route.ts:157 and :640), and its computed-filter path deliberately constrains before paginating so that count stays correct under a distance filter (:337). So the zero was a naming result, not a capability result, and this direction is much cheaper than I first said: one call per category, one per city, and a render site. I had it wrong in the expensive direction and it is worth correcting because it changes whether this is a project or an afternoon.

What I could not determine

Whether any of this converts: Mobbin is stills, so every claim above is a mechanism argument and none is a measured outcome. Whether Solen's real seeded photos actually discriminate between venues, which is the single unverified assumption the whole direction rests on, because if the photos do not distinguish one salon from another then more of them is more area and not more evidence (WHY_CURRENCY 5c, still open). And whether the counts are large enough to be worth printing at all: the anchor here is illustrative, and if the real Zurich number turns out to be small then this direction's own headline argues against using it, which is the honest test to run first, and it is now a one-line query rather than a build.

Direction G. A menu, not a search.

The bet. Home's job is to show what we can actually do, as a grid of named categories, because a text field asks the customer to guess a word we already own. Fresha proves this against itself: focus its web search field and the first thing it does is drop a menu of six categories under your cursor. If the field's opening move is to show a menu, the menu can be the field.

What it gives up. A grid cannot say "Saturday morning", and that is the one constraint that decides whether a booking exists at all, so anyone with a time need has to reach the search anyway and the search is now a 64px cell below the menu. It declares a taxonomy, so whatever is not a tile does not exist for a browsing user. It spends the top of the phone on four tiles, which is only affordable because the taxonomy turned out to be four. And it prints category counts, which publishes how thin our thinnest category is: that is the owner's call to make, not a design call.

The finding that changed the screen

The taxonomy is four, not six. CATEGORY_OPTIONS in lib/constants/categories.ts is typed to SalonCategory and holds coiffeur, barbershop, nails, spa. A fifth or sixth tile would have been a word no salon in the database can be filed under. That cuts both ways. Against the bet: four words are easy to guess, so the field was not asking much, and a four-item menu is a weaker replacement for a field than a twelve-item one. For the bet: four tiles fit, so the menu can lead and the first salon photographs still reach the first viewport, which is the collision named in WHY_RELATIONS I2 that should have killed this direction outright. It also forced the headline to be honest: this is a menu of shop types, not of things you want done. If the interesting layer is really the 67 rows in service_categories, then the menu should be built from services, and this screen is the sketch of a better version of itself.

One collision the owner has to settle

The card's rating row is caught between two live gates that cannot both be obeyed. CARD_REDESIGN_2026-07-13 C11 says row 1 is star plus value only, no review count. PSYCHOLOGY.md law 6 says a star with a bare average is forbidden and the count must sit beside it. Drawn per C11, because C11 is a dated owner decision about this exact row, and marked in the markup rather than silently resolved. Worth saying which side has the better argument on the merits: law 6 does, since 4.8 from three reviews and 4.8 from three hundred are different facts and the card cannot tell them apart. That is a call about honesty, not taste, so it should be yours.

What else the gates corrected, in both directions

The per-category count sub-line already existed and was deleted on 2026-07-16 for being fabricated: it claimed 42 salons for Coiffeur while the database held 20 in total. Counts are the load-bearing element of this direction, so they ship only behind the server-side count query that comment asks for. The 28 is real, read from the live snapshot. The 12/7/5/4 split is illustrative, and it is exactly the element that got deleted last time.

Draft two "fixed" the discount pill by copying the warm-red photo tag out of SalonCard.tsx, and the resurrection gate blocked it: that code is dormant and the owner rejected it twice. The live treatment is the pale-green pill beside the price. On the discount, the memory note was right and the component file was the stale artefact. Reading source is not proof a treatment renders.

Two stale references found on the way: taste rule 3 in CLAUDE.md still lists bracketed review counts as a legal blue accent, and the same category is named "Spa & Wellness" in SalonCard and searchCategories but differently in CATEGORY_OPTIONS. One entity, two names.

Two absences the screen refuses to hide

The seed photo set has no nails photography and no spa photography. So those two tiles and two cards render the FLOORS LAW 2 fallback. A menu built out of real inventory shows you where the inventory thins out, which is the honest version of "show off the stores that we have". Worth knowing: the law specs the fallback as sunken plus category icon plus initial, while SalonCard ships a monogram only. Drawn per the law, flagged rather than smoothed.

A third asset problem only the render exposed, which no measurement would have caught: the set holds exactly ONE barbershop photo, so the Barbershop tile and the barbershop card were showing the identical image one above the other. The card moved below the fold to break it. That is a patch, not a fix. A menu faced with real photography needs more photography than a feed does, because the same picture now has two jobs on one screen.

Where each part came from

  • Full-photo tiles with the label inside keep the category tile anatomically distinct from the salon card, which puts its text below the photo. Fresha runs the same split: a two-column category grid, and photo-top cards for salons.
  • Real counts on the tiles turn an index into proof of supply. Every product that does it prints the thin case too: Airbnb Services renders "5 available" and seven "Coming soon" tiles rather than hiding them, Depop labels two of its four rows with a count of one, Typeform prints "Rewards 1".
  • The exit as a member of the grid. Hers demotes search to the eighth tile and marks it by swapping the chevron for a magnifier. Goodreads prints the remainder as "Show 9 more categories" instead of a vague see-all. This cell does both jobs at once.
  • Location asserted, never asked. Square Go proves a location it never requested inside a section title; Google Maps writes "Latest in Downtown". So the guess is stated above the cards where it can be checked, with one small blue link to correct it.

Measured, not asserted

Rendered at 390 wide and read with getBoundingClientRect over the first 844px. Four distinct sizes (30, 20, 14, 12), so the ceiling of four is met exactly. Anchor 2.14x body, clearing the 1.8x floor. 24% of first-viewport text at weight 600, under the ~30% ceiling. Getting there cost two corrections the first render exposed: it measured 36%, so the wordmark and the rating value dropped to 500, which are the only two in the fold that no lock pins at 600. A touch target measured 38px and is now 44. Geometry: exit cell at y=413, first salon card at y=567, page 1387 tall.

The floor this direction misses, stated plainly: photographic area is 28% of the first viewport, against the ~1/3 imagery floor. Four photos reach the fold (two category tiles, two card images) and a fifth is cropped at the edge. The gap is structural, not a spacing tweak: half the category tiles have no photography to show, and closing it means either moving the menu below the feed the way Fresha does, or shooting nails and spa work. It should not be closed by adding a decorative image, which is the thing rejected three times.

One more conflict, surfaced not smoothed: the price sits at weight 400 in the meta line per this brief, which contradicts FLOORS LAW 6's "price 600 tabular" two-anchor rule. The brief is newer, so it wins here.

Direction H. The next free day.

The bet. Home's job is to turn "I need a haircut this week" into a held appointment, so the screen is organised on one axis, when, in two places: a rail that sets the earliest day you are willing to go, and one row per card naming that salon's own soonest bookable day. The feed is sorted soonest first, which is why the day words run down the page as a column: today, today, tomorrow, tomorrow, Sat, Mon. That column is the argument. Nothing here ranks salons by anything except how soon you can sit down.

What it gives up

  • Card count, and there is no way around it. Measured on the render at 390x844, not estimated: each card is 328px tall, the feed starts at y=226, and the first viewport holds one complete card plus the whole photo of the second, about 1.6 content units against a density floor of 4 on mobile. The band is gone, not empty. The feed moves up to fill the space, so a first timer gets a shorter page and never a hole where a card they cannot have would have been. This is the state that decides the whole direction, and it is the cheapest one to get wrong.

The bet. There is exactly one home screen for everybody. Personalisation happens in one band under the search field and nowhere else, so a first timer sees a shorter page rather than a different one. Four states, one page: has booked here before, has an appointment coming up, has only looked around, brand new. The chrome above the band and the feed below it are byte-identical in all four.

What the chrome gives up to hold that line. The greeting goes. The live Hero renders a name line above the headline, and a greeting is personalisation sitting in the chrome, which means two homes again the moment it appears. The account control stays an icon and never becomes an avatar photograph, for the same reason. The search field stays a prompt reading Salon, service or city even when the last query is known, so it never becomes a state display. The Hero sub line goes too, because the band needs the vertical space more than a second sentence does.

What the direction gives up, stated plainly. It is the least visible bet of the set in a screenshot, because the top of the page looks the same to every visitor and none of the design effort shows. It permanently forecloses the loud version, where a returning customer gets an account-shaped home with their own numbers on it, so their home can only ever be slightly better than a newcomer's. It multiplies home from one state to four, each of which has to be specified, seeded and graded. It needs a real bookings and history read, so it pays nothing at launch and cannot be faked. And it inherits the failure mode of every conditional section: sparse but present reads as a bug in a way absence never does, which is why the band is a full-width row list and not a fixed-width rail, and why the empty state here is deletion rather than a placeholder.

The one collapse it accepts. The mobile entry control goes from three stacked segments to one row, which drops the visible date field. That field is not decoration: it is the constraint that decides whether a result can exist at all. The trade is deliberate. The row opens the existing overlay, which already owns service, city and date as segment tabs, so the constraint moves one tap away rather than disappearing. If you want the date on the surface, take it from the band's vertical budget and say so, because the fold cannot pay for both.

The findings behind it, read from the lens files rather than re-observed here. WHY_ENTRY bet E3, the only bet in that file held by three independent products, is that the control stays constant while the slot beneath it knows who you are: Zocdoc holds one search field unchanged across three captures of the same screen and swaps the card under it, Airbnb keeps a pill reading Start your search while the abandoned query sits below it as a dismissible card, and Fresha's first home section is Book again with a named salon. WHY_RETURN position D adds the absence half: Expedia's home without a recents row does not read as broken, and the Starbucks home at zero stars simply has no Usuals row. Its third capture is the load-bearing one, recents shown to a signed-out session, which is why state three here is triggered by history and not by sign-in. WHY_RETURN also names the discriminator this direction depends on, that Solen's returning customer returns to a person on a four to eight week cycle rather than to a category, so the band's default content is Book again and not Continue searching. WHY_RELATIONS I1 supplied the shape: the corpus resolution for a personal block competing with a photo-first feed is not to pick one, it is to strip the personal block's photograph down to a left thumbnail, which is what Marriott and Fresha both do and what keeps the imagery floor cheap here.

What is still open, and only you can close it

Eight directions is not eight things to build. Most of them are eliminated by two or three answers, so here are the answers, in the order that eliminates the most.

The five that decide it

  • 1. What is the biggest thing on a card a picture of? The venue (E, F, G, H, K and L all assume it), the stylist (I), or the finished work (J). This is the choice that decides the most, because it commits photography, a data model, a backend read and the component graph, and none of those is an afternoon. Answer it and I and J live or die immediately.
  • 2. Does the first screen have to contain real salons? Yes eliminates E. No eliminates F. It is the runner-up to question 1 and it is genuinely close, but every one of its consequences is a layout edge, and layout is the cheapest thing in this estate to change.
  • 3. May a bookable day go on a card? This re-opens an owner-approved deletion from 2026-07-13, so it is your call alone. It is also wider than one row: because our imagery lock makes the photo the largest element, a day forces the card count down, which collides with our own six-desktop density floor. H exists to be answered, not to be built.
  • 4. Do we publish per-category supply counts? F and G both rest on them. A count is only evidence if it is printed when it is small, so this commits us to showing how thin the thinnest Swiss category currently is. That is a business disclosure decision rather than a design one, which is why it is on this list instead of settled inside a mockup.
  • 5. Is rebooking allowed on home at all? K needs an explicit yes, because a barber-era express-rebook widget sits in the graveyard from 2026-07-11. It was deleted for a narrow reason, an orphaned widget with no importers, and a narrow reason is still not permission. The backend is fully live with zero customer render site.

Three things the mockups cannot answer, so nobody should pretend they did

  • Whether our venue photographs actually discriminate between venues. A photo earns the biggest slot on a card by telling two salons apart. If a Basel Coiffeur interior and the one two streets away photograph nearly identically, question 1 mostly collapses and question 2 wins by default. Nobody has measured it, and it is worth more than any further sweep.
  • Anything about motion. The whole corpus is still images and all eight screens here are static. No transition, easing, gesture or press state was observed, and none is claimed.
  • Whether any of this converts. There is no engagement, retention or conversion data anywhere in the research. Every "buys" and "costs" is a mechanism argument, which is stronger than a frequency count and weaker than a test.

One thing to know before you read the screens

Three of these are asks, not proposals. H re-opens a settled deletion, K needs a graveyard yes, and F and G both need a supply-count query that does not exist yet plus your decision to publish thin numbers. The other four could be built on what already ships. Every screen is static: no motion, no hover, no interaction, and the photographs are the real seeded salon set rather than stock.
