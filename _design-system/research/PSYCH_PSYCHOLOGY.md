# UI/UX Psychology Research Synthesis for Solen.ch

## Top principles (ranked)

**1. Peak-end rule dominates flow memory**: People judge an entire experience by its most intense moment and its final moment, not average quality or duration. Evidence: Redelmeier and Kahneman's colonoscopy study found pain ratings correlated near-zero with duration (r=.03) but strongly with peak and final-3-minute pain ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duration_neglect)). Application: the Solen booking confirmation screen after payment carries disproportionate memory weight versus the 4 steps before it. Smallest lever: invest polish specifically in the confirmation screen's tone/clarity (brief success moment, no upsell stacked on it), not in shaving steps elsewhere. Confidence: high.

**2. Defaults capture the majority who would not otherwise decide**: Switching a choice from opt-in to opt-out dramatically raises uptake of the default. Evidence: Johnson and Goldstein (2003, Science), organ-donor consent rose from 42% to 82% purely from opt-out framing ([paper](https://www.dangoldstein.com/papers/JohnsonGoldstein_Defaults_Transplantation2004.pdf)). Application: staff-selection and date/time steps in booking. Smallest lever: pre-select "any available staff" and the next available time slot, but never pre-check anything that costs money or commits to recurring billing (that crosses into the dark-pattern version of the same mechanism). Confidence: high.

**3. Endowed progress and goal-gradient acceleration drive loyalty completion**: A head start toward a goal, even with identical remaining effort, increases completion rate and speeds it up, because motivation tracks proportion-complete, not absolute distance. Evidence: Nunes and Dreze (2006, JCR), car-wash stamp cards: 0-of-8 got 19% redemption, 2-of-10 (same 8 needed) got 34% ([paper](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/32/4/504/1787425)); Kivetz, Urminsky and Zheng (2006, JMR) replicated with coffee cards, 12.7 vs 15.6 days to completion ([paper](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1509/jmkr.43.1.39)). Application: Solen Status (Base/Gold/Platinum). Smallest lever: credit new signups with 1 of N bookings already done (with a stated reason, e.g. "welcome bonus"), never show a literal 0 bar; on tier-up, immediately seed partial progress toward the next tier instead of resetting to zero. Confidence: high.

**4. First 50-100ms decide trust, and that snap judgment barely updates**: Aesthetic and facial-trustworthiness judgments form near-instantly and anchor everything after. Evidence: Lindgaard et al. (2006) found 50ms exposure ratings correlated r≈.90 with longer exposure ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/first-impressions-human-automaticity/)); Willis and Todorov (2006) found trustworthiness judged reliably from 100ms face exposure, plateauing by 167ms ([paper](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01750.x)). Application: salon card first image, home screen first paint. Smallest lever: prioritize fast, high-quality first paint of hero/card photos over secondary chrome; no blurry placeholder lingering in that window.

**5. Photos are the primary decision input, not decoration**: 56% of shoppers' first action on a product page is looking at images before any text. Evidence: Baymard Institute large-scale usability benchmarking ([Baymard](https://baymard.com/blog/current-state-ecommerce-product-page-ux)); reinforced by Airbnb's professional-photo rollout showing ~17.5% booking lift ([case study](https://www.lemonone.com/expert-page/how-much-is-an-image-worth-what-can-we-learn-from-airbnb)). Application: salon cards and PDP gallery. Smallest lever: load highest-resolution photo first, no lazy-blur lingering, photo stays the largest single element on the card.

**6. Review count matters as much as the average score, and a perfect 5.0 is suspicious**: Purchase likelihood peaks around 4.0-4.7 and declines approaching 5.0; most users pick a lower-rated item with more reviews over a near-perfect one with almost none. Evidence: Spiegel Research Center, Northwestern ([source](https://spiegel.medill.northwestern.edu/how-online-reviews-influence-sales/)); Baymard survey found 56-62% chose the higher-review-count option over the higher-score/low-count one ([Baymard](https://baymard.com/blog/sort-by-customer-ratings)). Application: SalonCard and PDP rating display, any "top rated" sort logic. Smallest lever: never sort/badge by raw score alone, weight by score x review count so a thin 5.0 doesn't outrank a well-reviewed 4.4; always show count next to the star, never a bare average.

**7. Hidden costs revealed late are the single largest checkout-abandonment driver**: Drip pricing (fees shown only at the final step) accounts for roughly 21% of cart abandonment. Evidence: Baymard Institute checkout research ([Baymard](https://baymard.com/learn/checkout-flow-ux-optimization)). Application: Solen's pay step, where any platform fee or surcharge could otherwise appear late. Smallest lever: show the full total, including any fee, at the moment service and staff are selected (start of flow), not only at Bezahlen.

**8. Loss aversion is asymmetric, roughly 2x stronger than equivalent gains, though the exact ratio is contested**: Kahneman and Tversky's prospect theory established the concave/convex, steeper-on-losses value function; a 2019 meta-analysis found the 2:1 ratio is not a universal constant ([NCBI](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6902077/)). Application: credits/promo banners, loyalty status-at-risk messaging. Smallest lever: frame a real, imminent loss ("credits expire, apply now") rather than fabricate one; frame a status at risk of downgrade as a loss when the user is closer to losing current tier than gaining the next.

**9. Scarcity and urgency cues measurably raise stress and frustration, and fabricated ones cause reactance and brand-switching intent when detected**: Tuncer et al. (2023) found countdown timers raised frustration (NASA-TLX) and lowered perceived vendor benevolence even while lifting short-term intent ([source](https://uxpsychology.substack.com/p/the-use-of-scarcity-cues-in-e-commerce)); Biraglia and Ilenia (2021, Psychology & Marketing) found perceived-inauthentic scarcity triggers anger and switching intent ([paper](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/mar.21489)); the OneTravel fake-viewer-count widget went viral when its randomness was discovered ([case](https://www.nudgify.com/fake-social-proof/)). Application: any "X people booked today" or slot-scarcity element. Smallest lever: never simulate a count; only show real, server-computed numbers, and this already matches Solen's locked no-fabrication rule, so the lever is enforcement, not new design.

**10. Skeleton screens raise perceived speed over spinners, but evidence is mixed and effect is not universal**: Mejtoft et al. (2018) found skeleton screens scored higher on perceived speed and ease of navigation ([NN/g summary](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/skeleton-screens/)), but a 2017 Viget study found skeletons performed worst on perceived duration, and first-visit findability was actually better with spinners in the same 2018 study. Application: salon PDP and search results loading states. Smallest lever: keep Solen's existing layout-matching Skeleton component for full-page/module loads (2-10s), keep plain spinners for small in-page fetches (promo code validation), and don't treat skeleton as a guaranteed universal win.

**11. Response-time thresholds are well-established and dictate feedback design, not just performance targets**: 0.1s feels instant, 1s is the ceiling for uninterrupted flow, 10s is the ceiling before users mentally disengage and need a real progress indicator. Evidence: Nielsen's three response-time limits, restated by NN/g ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/response-times-3-important-limits/)). Application: search filter apply, salon PDP load, TWINT/card payment confirmation. Smallest lever: if payment confirmation can exceed 10 seconds, replace a bare spinner with staged copy ("Verbindung wird hergestellt" then "Zahlung wird bestätigt").

**12. Hick's law means collapsing many options into few categories beats trimming an already-short list**: Decision time scales with log2(number of options), so going from 16 to 8 barely helps, but 8 to 2 helps a lot. Evidence: classic Hick-Hyman reaction-time research, summarized by NN/g and Laws of UX ([source](https://lawsofux.com/hicks-law/)). Important caveat baked into the same evidence base: the "more choices reduce conversion" jam-study effect does NOT reliably replicate at the population level (Scheibehenne et al. 2010 meta-analysis, d≈0.02 across 5,036 subjects, [paper](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/37/3/409/1827647)); overload only reliably appears when options are hard to compare or the chooser lacks expertise (Chernev et al. 2015, [paper](https://myscp.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1016/j.jcps.2014.08.002)). Application: salon detail page services list. Smallest lever: group services into 3-5 labeled categories (mere categorization itself lifts satisfaction per Mogilner, Rudnick and Iyengar 2008) and show price/duration inline so options are easy to compare, rather than blanket-capping list length.

**13. Touch targets have a hard physical floor, not just a design guideline**: Minimum 44x44pt/9-10mm is required for fast, accurate one-handed thumb selection; below this floor, fingertip contact-patch imprecision (not carefulness) becomes the dominant error source. Evidence: Parhi, Karlson and Bederson (MobileHCI 2006, Microsoft Research, [paper](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2006/01/parhi-mobileHCI06.pdf)); Bi, Li and Zhai's FFitts Law (CHI 2013) confirmed a separate, size-independent finger-imprecision error term ([paper](https://research.google/pubs/ffitts-law-modeling-finger-touch-with-fitts-law/)). Application: time-slot buttons, dismiss-X controls, favorite hearts. Smallest lever: enforce 44pt minimum hit area (padding-expanded, not just the glyph) on every tappable element, especially inline dismiss/remove controls next to other tappable elements.

**14. IKEA effect only pays off on completed, voluntary tasks, and forcing setup before value backfires**: Self-built items are valued as highly as expert-made ones, but only when the build is completed and kept; abandoned or destroyed builds get none of the premium. Evidence: Norton, Mochon and Ariely (2012, J. Consumer Psychology, [paper](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1057740811000829)). NN/g explicitly warns against gating core value behind mandatory customization ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/increase-user-autonomy/)). Application: Hair DNA / preference profile builder. Smallest lever: let the customer build the profile via visible tap-to-choose actions, but keep it optional and deferred to after the first successful booking, not a gate before search.

**15. Single-column, minimal-field forms convert better, and forced account creation at commit is a major abandonment driver**: single-column forms completed 15.4s faster with statistical significance (CXL/Speero, 702 participants, [study](https://speero.com/post/form-field-usability-should-you-use-single-or-multi-column-forms-original-research)); Baymard found 24% of shoppers abandoned specifically due to forced account creation (n=4,384, [Baymard](https://baymard.com/blog/make-guest-checkout-prominent)). Application: the Bezahlen step. Smallest lever: single-column stacked fields only; if any step still hard-gates full account creation before payment, replace with a phone/OTP field that doubles as the account.

## Contested / myth-check

- **"More options always reduce conversion" (the jam-study/paradox-of-choice headline claim) is overstated.** What IS supported: a 2010 meta-analysis of 50 experiments (N=5,036) found the average effect is near zero (d≈0.02) with huge study-to-study variance ([Scheibehenne et al.](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/37/3/409/1827647)); overload reliably appears only under specific moderators, high option complexity, hard-to-compare options, low chooser expertise, and time pressure (Chernev, Bockenholt and Goodman 2015, [paper](https://myscp.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1016/j.jcps.2014.08.002)). Do not blanket-cap Solen's service or staff lists on the strength of Iyengar's jam study alone; fix comparability and reduce complexity instead of hiding options.

- **Skeleton screens as a guaranteed perceived-speed win is contested.** A 2018 peer-reviewed study found skeletons beat spinners on perceived speed but spinners won on first-visit findability ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/326858669_The_effect_of_skeleton_screens_Users'_perception_of_speed_and_ease_of_navigation)); a separate 2017 Viget study found skeletons performed worst on perceived duration. What IS supported: use skeletons for full-page/module loads matching final layout (NN/g's own scoping), not as a blanket replacement for spinners everywhere.

- **The Zeigarnik effect (better memory for unfinished tasks) does not reliably replicate.** A 2025 systematic review/meta-analysis found no consistent memory advantage for incomplete tasks, but DID confirm the separate Ovsiankina effect (a general behavioral tendency to want to resume interrupted tasks) ([Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-025-05000-w)). What IS supported: design for resumption (save state, show a "Continue booking" affordance), not for "users will remember the gap" copy.

- **The left-digit pricing effect ($39 vs $40) is real but conflicts with Swiss round-pricing convention and Solen's own no-fake-precision copy rule.** Anderson and Simester's field experiment is solid evidence ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/23547242_Penny_Wise_and_Pound_Foolish_The_Left-Digit_Effect_in_Price_Cognition)), but this is a genuine values trade-off, not a lever to pull. Flag, do not silently introduce .90/.95 endings.

- **"Most popular" badges lifting selection by 25-40%, LinkedIn's 55-100%+ profile-completion lift, Drift's "doubled" onboarding conversion, HubSpot's 59.2% multi-step form lift, and the $429-anchor-doubles-sales bread-maker story are all vendor/practitioner self-reports, not controlled studies.** Directionally plausible, mechanism-consistent with better-evidenced principles (endowed progress, anchoring), but treat exact percentages as marketing-blog noise, not data to cite in specs.

- **Booking.com's "20-30% conversion lift from psychological triggers" and TaskRabbit's "28% higher conversion from verification badges" are single-company/practitioner claims with no isolated effect size for the specific tactic.** The underlying mechanisms (social proof, verification trust) are supported elsewhere with better evidence; these specific numbers are not.

- **Genuine urgency outperforming fake/resetting urgency ("2-3x" claim) has no peer-reviewed source, only converging industry blog claims.** The mechanism (users detect timer resets via refresh) is directionally sound engineering caution, but the magnitude is unverified.

- **The accelerating-progress-bar-feels-faster claim (Harrison et al., Microsoft Research) is a real study**, but a related NN/g claim that this generalizes broadly to all progress bars was not traced to a primary source in the research; treat the loyalty-card goal-gradient result (Kivetz et al.) as the strong evidence and the progress-bar-acceleration-as-UI-trick as a weaker, adjacent claim.

- **"Repeated exposure to urgency triggers causes habituation/fatigue" and "gaze-cued photos pull attention toward products" are both real but thin.** The habituation claim comes from a low-rigor secondary source with no effect size; the gaze-cueing claim is real eye-tracking research but its extension to Solen's photo curation is an inference, not a tested application.

- **Pinterest's "diversity-aware ranking improves engagement" and Duolingo's "+14% D7 retention from streak wagers" are single-company self-reported case studies**, useful directionally for Inspo feed design but not independently replicated.
