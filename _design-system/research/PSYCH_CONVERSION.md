# Research compendium: conversion optimization (fleet 2b, 2026-07-07)

<!-- exists-check: net-new, part of _design-system/research/ evidence base (workstream 11) -->

Produced by 20 sonnet web-researchers (one subtopic each) + 1 synthesizer, workflow wf_7d6fab6f-13a (20/20 researchers returned, 147 raw findings). Cross-check contested items against VIDEO_UXPEAK_PSYCHOLOGY.md claim-check.

## Top principles (ranked)

1. **No forced account creation before payment**: Requiring signup before purchase is one of the largest single named causes of checkout abandonment. Evidence: Baymard Institute checkout usability research, 18-26% of shoppers abandon over forced account creation ([baymard.com](https://baymard.com/blog/current-state-of-checkout-ux)), reinforced by NN/g's login-wall case comparisons ([nngroup.com](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/login-walls/)). Solen application: keep search, salon PDP, services, staff, time selection fully usable while logged out; only require phone/email at Bezahlen, defer full account/password creation to the confirmation screen. Smallest lever: move any existing signup prompt from pre-Bezahlen to post-payment. Confidence: high.

2. **Show the true total early, never surprise costs at the last step**: Unexpected costs revealed late are the single largest driver of cart/booking abandonment (roughly 39-48% of abandoners cite this). Evidence: Baymard Institute large-scale checkout research ([baymard.com](https://baymard.com/blog/ecommerce-checkout-usability-report-and-benchmark)). Solen application: show final CHF total (service price plus any surcharge, promo, credit) on every booking step, not just at Bezahlen. Smallest lever: repeat the running total in the header/footer of every booking screen. Confidence: high.

3. **Cut and autofill payment/contact form fields**: More form fields directly lowers checkout completion; average checkout has 11.3 fields but top performers use 8. Evidence: Baymard Institute field-count benchmark ([baymard.com](https://baymard.com/blog/checkout-flow-average-form-fields)), reinforced by Zuko's autofill study showing 71% vs 59% completion with autofill triggered ([zuko.io](https://www.zuko.io/blog/does-browser-autofill-affect-form-conversion-rate)). Solen application: audit the Bezahlen step, drop redundant name/phone fields already in profile, use one full-name field (not split), correct autocomplete/inputmode tokens on phone and card fields. Smallest lever: add inputmode="numeric"/type="tel" and proper autocomplete attributes to every payment/contact input. Confidence: high.

4. **Smart, non-blank defaults on staff and time selection**: Defaults dramatically increase the share of users who end up choosing that option, replicated at large scale (d=0.68, n=73,675). Evidence: Cambridge Behavioural Public Policy meta-analysis of defaults ([cambridge.org](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/behavioural-public-policy/article/when-and-why-defaults-influence-decisions-a-metaanalysis-of-default-effects/67AF6972CFB52698A60B6BD94B70C2C0)), and Madrian and Shea's 401k field study ([nber.org](https://www.nber.org/papers/w7682)). Solen application: pre-select "Anyone" as default staff and highlight the earliest available time slot as a selectable default in the Zeit step, rather than forcing an explicit tap on a blank state. Smallest lever: default-select the "Anyone" staff chip and pre-highlight the soonest slot. Confidence: high.

5. **Always show review count next to the star rating**: Users distrust a bare star average and prefer a slightly lower rating backed by many reviews over a near-perfect rating with few. Evidence: Baymard Institute, 5 surveys, 5,170+ respondents, nearly 2x preferred 4.5 stars/57 reviews over 5 stars/4 reviews ([baymard.com](https://baymard.com/blog/user-perception-of-product-ratings)); corroborated by Spiegel Research Center finding purchase likelihood peaks at 4.0-4.7 and drops near 5.0 ([spiegel.medill.northwestern.edu](https://spiegel.medill.northwestern.edu/how-online-reviews-influence-sales/)). Solen application: never show a rounded/whole-star badge alone; pair rating with count "4.8 (54)" on every card and the PDP. Smallest lever: confirm the review count renders next to every star badge, including on search cards. Confidence: high.

6. **Price must be visible on every listing card, consistently positioned**: Absent or inconsistently placed price/rating/distance attributes make users distrust or discard listings rather than click through. Evidence: Baymard Institute product-list usability testing, 64% of sites fail consistent attribute presentation, 40% fail visual distinction between elements ([baymard.com](https://baymard.com/blog/list-item-design-ecommerce), [baymard.com](https://baymard.com/blog/product-listing-information)). Solen application: keep price, rating, and distance in the same fixed card slot on every SalonCard, with a neutral placeholder when data is missing (new salon, no location permission) rather than collapsing the layout. Smallest lever: audit SalonCard for any conditional layout shift when rating/distance is absent. Confidence: high.

7. **List view default on mobile search, not map-first**: Maps on mobile cause gesture conflicts (pan vs scroll) and tiny unselectable pins; list lets users scan faster, and users rarely miss a map when it's absent. Evidence: NN/g mobile usability testing ([nngroup.com](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mobile-maps-locations/)); on desktop, a persistent split view (list+map together) drove 95% map engagement vs low usage in tabbed layouts, per Baymard ([baymard.com](https://baymard.com/blog/accommodations-split-view)). Solen application: keep list as the default view on mobile web and iOS; on desktop/tablet consider a persistent split layout instead of a full-screen map toggle. Smallest lever: verify mobile search never auto-opens map view, and any map toggle preserves filters/scroll position on return. Confidence: high.

8. **Progress indicator across the booking flow, sequential and non-skippable**: Visible progress reduces confusion and increases both willingness to continue and to wait; NN/g and Baymard converge on this even though the size of the effect is only an estimated range. Evidence: NN/g wizard design guidance ([nngroup.com](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/wizards/)); Baymard's synthesis of 100+ checkout studies estimates +5-15% completion impact from a clear step indicator ([baymard.com](https://baymard.com/blog/current-state-of-checkout-ux)). Solen application: keep the 4-step icon-stepper (Services/Staff/Zeit/Bezahlen) visible and enforce sequential order (no skipping ahead to Bezahlen before Zeit is chosen). Smallest lever: confirm the stepper always renders 1:1 with the real 4 steps and later steps stay locked until earlier ones complete. Confidence: medium-high.

9. **Visible trust cue right at card entry, using a recognizable brand mark**: Nearly 1 in 5 shoppers abandon over payment-data trust; recognizable logos (Stripe, TWINT) outperform generic padlocks because users judge by brand recognition, not actual security. Evidence: Baymard Institute payment-form trust research, seal-recognition study n=3,516 ([baymard.com](https://baymard.com/blog/perceived-security-of-payment-form)). Solen application: place the Stripe logo plus a short reassurance line directly beside the card-number field (not footer-only); show TWINT and card as visible tappable rows with logos, not a dropdown. Smallest lever: add the Stripe wordmark next to the card field. Confidence: medium-high.

10. **Collapse the promo code field behind a link, don't show it empty by default**: An empty visible coupon field signals users are overpaying and prompts code-hunting departures (27% say they'd abandon to search for a code). Evidence: PayPal/Comscore study via Zuko ([zuko.io](https://www.zuko.io/blog/are-discount-coupon-codes-a-good-idea-for-your-ecommerce-checkout)), Baymard's checkout research on coupon field visibility ([baymard.com](https://baymard.com/blog/checkout-usability-apply-buttons)). Caveat: GoodUI's replicated 10-test aggregate found only a non-significant +0.5% effect, so treat as low-risk plausible upside, not guaranteed lift ([goodui.org](https://goodui.org/patterns/1/)). Solen application: collapse the promo code input behind a "Promo-Code?" text link that expands on tap; keep an explicit Apply button (this is the one field where Baymard recommends it) and clearly distinguish it from the Guthaben/credits line. Smallest lever: change the default-visible empty box to a collapsed link. Confidence: medium.

11. **Empty/no-availability states need one concrete next action, not passive text**: A well-formed empty state states what's happening, teaches what belongs there, and gives one direct clickable path; nearly half of sites give no effective recovery from zero results. Evidence: Baymard Institute no-results research ([baymard.com](https://baymard.com/blog/no-results-page)), NN/g empty-state guidelines ([nngroup.com](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/empty-state-interface-design/)). Solen application: when city+category+date filters zero out, auto-relax the date filter first and surface a specific clickable alternative (a real nearby date or city), not generic "try broadening your search" copy. Smallest lever: replace generic empty-state copy with one computed, tappable alternative. Confidence: medium-high.

12. **Endowed/visible progress for loyalty status and booking-flow progress bar**: People complete goals faster and at higher rates when progress is pre-filled versus starting from zero, even for identical remaining effort. Evidence: Nunes and Dreze (2006) car-wash stamp-card field experiment, 34% vs 19% redemption ([journals.sagepub.com](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1509/jmkr.43.1.39)); Kivetz, Urminsky, Zheng (2006) found purchase pace accelerates near a reward. Solen application: show loyalty tier progress starting from a nonzero baseline (e.g. a welcome credit toward Gold) rather than 0 bookings; give the booking progress bar a small filled sliver on step 1 instead of empty. Smallest lever: pre-fill the loyalty progress bar with a starting credit. Confidence: high (for the mechanism; medium for exact Solen sizing).

13. **Sticky/persistent booking CTA on the salon detail page**: A CTA that stays visible through scroll increases completed transactions, replicated across independent tests though individual studies are often underpowered. Evidence: Growth Rock A/B test, +7.9% completed orders at 99% significance ([growthrock.co](https://growthrock.co/sticky-add-to-cart-button-example/)); GoodUI's pooled meta-analysis of 28 tests reached only 88.4% of target power, so treat as a real but moderate-confidence pattern, not a guaranteed headline win ([goodui.org](https://goodui.org/patterns/41/)). Solen application: keep the primary Buchen CTA fixed at the bottom of the salon detail page through the full scroll of photos, reviews, and services. Smallest lever: verify the CTA is position:sticky/fixed on the PDP, not just anchored at top or bottom of static content. Confidence: medium.

14. **Reduce interaction cost, not step count, in the booking flow**: What drives abandonment is field/task load per step, not the number of pages; a same-fields single-page vs multi-step split shows little difference, and Baymard explicitly found few navigation problems between steps. Evidence: Baymard Institute, Christian Holst's one-page-checkout analysis ([baymard.com](https://baymard.com/blog/one-page-checkout)); reinforced by aggregated multi-step form data (13.85% vs 4.53% conversion, industry aggregate, directional only) ([leadgen-economy.com](https://www.leadgen-economy.com/blog/multi-step-forms-conversion-optimization/)). Solen application: keep the 4-step flow (services, staff, time, pay) as is; do not collapse to one page or split into a 5th step; instead keep each step to one decision with minimal fields. Smallest lever: audit each booking step for any field/decision that doesn't belong to that step's core task. Confidence: high.

15. **Verified-purchase badges on reviews increase trust and perceived positivity**: Reviews tied to a verified transaction are rated more credible and lift purchase probability, and Solen already has this data since every review originates from a completed booking. Evidence: Spiegel Research Center (Northwestern), verified-buyer badge lifted purchase probability 15% ([spiegel.medill.northwestern.edu](https://spiegel.medill.northwestern.edu/how-online-reviews-influence-sales/)); corroborated by NN/g trust research on third-party vs self-curated content ([nngroup.com](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/trustworthy-design/)). Solen application: add a small "Verified booking" tag on each review card. Smallest lever: surface the existing booking-linkage as a visible badge on review cards. Confidence: medium-high.

## Contested / myth-check

- **"Choice overload" / the jam study does not generally replicate.** The famous 6-vs-24-jam result (30% vs 3% purchase) is real but the broader literature does not support "fewer options always convert better." Scheibehenne, Greifeneder and Todd's meta-analysis of 50 studies (n=5,036) found an overall effect close to zero ([academic.oup.com](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/37/3/409/1827647)). What IS supported: good defaults (rule 4 above) help; artificially trimming the staff or time-slot list to "reduce choice" is not evidence-backed.

- **Vendor-reported Zenoti booking-abandonment stats ("71% abandon if slow", "8x conversion from 5-min response", "78% vs 39% return rate for online bookers") are single-vendor, self-reported, non-controlled figures** ([zenoti.com](https://www.zenoti.com/thecheckin/salon-medspa-booking-flow-revenue)). Directionally plausible but should not be cited as hard benchmarks. What IS supported: the general direction (fast, frictionless booking helps retention) aligns with better-evidenced principles above (defaults, fewer fields).

- **The "$300M button" / ASOS guest-checkout anecdotes are widely retold marketing case studies, not controlled experiments**, with inconsistent numbers across retellings ([thecodecreative.com](https://www.thecodecreative.com/blog/the-300-million-dollar-button), [econsultancy.com](https://econsultancy.com/why-has-asos-removed-its-guest-checkout-option/)). What IS supported: the underlying direction (forced signup hurts, guest checkout helps) is independently confirmed by Baymard's disclosed-methodology survey data (principle 1 above), so the direction is solid even though these specific case-study numbers aren't.

- **Charm pricing (.99 endings) lift claims of 24-60% come from aggregator/SEO content, not controlled studies**, and effect size is explicitly flagged as varying by tier and B2B vs B2C ([price2spy.com](https://www.price2spy.com/blog/charm-pricing/)). It also conflicts with the Swiss round-CHF pricing convention that signals trust in mid-market beauty. Do not apply mechanically. What IS supported: the left-digit effect itself (CHF 49 perceived as meaningfully cheaper than CHF 50) is a well-replicated cognitive bias (Thomas and Morwitz 2005), just don't expect the exaggerated retail lift numbers.

- **Scarcity messaging ("only 1 slot left") is contested on trust grounds** even though the underlying academic study (Teubner and Graul 2020, ScienceDirect) is real and peer-reviewed. It only works, and does not backfire, when the count is genuinely live and tied to real capacity. This directly conflicts with Solen's own no-fabricated-data rule; fabricated or stale scarcity counts should never ship. Treat as conditional, not a default UI pattern.

- **"Foot-in-the-door" compliance (Freedman and Fraser 1966, 76% lift) is a single 1966 study**; later meta-analytic work (Burger 1999) found the effect real but smaller and inconsistent, and it's moderated by individual differences in preference-for-consistency (Maksim and Spiewak 2023, [journals.sagepub.com](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/19485506221086176)). Don't assume one micro-commitment sequence works universally; treat as A/B-test material, not a settled pattern.

- **The "GoodUI extra checkout step +5.5%" test bundled multiple simultaneous UI changes** (button color, alignment, progress bar) and GoodUI itself assigned only 0.25/1 certainty ([goodui.org](https://goodui.org/blog/evidence-66-a-possible-5-5-to-sales-from-an-extra-checkout-step/)). Do not use this as license to add a 5th booking step. What IS supported: step count itself is a secondary variable; field/task load per step is the primary one (principle 14).

- **Unsourced content-farm rating-lift claims ("25% lift per 0.1 stars", "15-30% CTR from ratings") should be discarded entirely** ([powerreviews.com](https://www.powerreviews.com/average-rating-impact-on-conversion/)); numbers are inconsistent across sources with no disclosed methodology. Use the Baymard review-count finding (principle 5) instead, which has a disclosed sample and methodology.

- **"Best time to send abandoned-cart email = 1 hour" is an unattributed number repeated across marketing-vendor blogs** with no sample size, control, or methodology ever disclosed ([drip.com](https://www.drip.com/blog/abandoned-cart-email-timing)). Do not hardcode; A/B test on Solen's own data.

- **GoodUI's price-size/contrast test on listing cards (Finn.com, n=55,293) reached only 5.3% statistical power**, the weakest result in their listing-test set ([goodui.org](https://goodui.org/tests/screen/listing/)). Do not treat as a proven lever; Solen's current locked price hierarchy (bold-ink, smaller than name) should not be changed on this evidence.

- **"Speculation Rules API prerendering doubled conversion" is a single uncontrolled case study** ([nitropack.io](https://nitropack.io/blog/improve-conversion-rates-cwv/)). The mechanism (prefetching) is sound engineering practice regardless, but the doubling figure shouldn't be treated as an expected outcome.

- **TWINT-first-default conversion lift figures (82% vs 75%, "up to 15%") come from payment-processor marketing pages** (TWINT, Checkout.com, Adyen), not independent studies ([twint.ch](https://www.twint.ch/en/business-customers/products/twint-for-online-shops/)). The underlying fact that TWINT has high Swiss penetration (~60%) is well-established and worth acting on; the precise conversion percentages are not.

- **Baymard's "22% fewer errors from inline validation" and "positive inline validation" specifics are a third-party paraphrase**, not a Baymard-published number ([baymard.com](https://baymard.com/blog/inline-form-validation)). The directional finding (validate on blur, not just on submit) is still well-supported qualitatively.
