
# Visual Hierarchy Diagnosis — Research Delta

## Findings (ordered by usefulness)

1. **Scanning is task-optimized, not a fixed shape.** The same page produces radically different eye movement depending on the user's goal: a price-finding task took 28 fixations, a study-for-quiz task on the identical page took 228. "Users fixate on those elements on the page that are relevant for their task." Diagnosing hierarchy requires stating the task first, not judging the layout in the abstract. [source: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/eyetracking-tasks-efficient-scanning/] T1

2. **The F-shaped pattern is a *failure mode* of unformatted text, not a design target.** NN/g's original 2006 eyetracking study (232 users, thousands of pages) found two horizontal sweeps + a left vertical sweep, but the follow-up correction states the F only appears when "a page or a section of a page includes text that has little or no formatting for the web," and explicitly: "Good design can prevent F-shape scanning." Treat an F-shaped heatmap as a symptom of missing hierarchy, not a pattern to design toward. [source: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/f-shaped-pattern-reading-web-content-discovered/] [source: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/f-shaped-pattern-reading-web-content/] T1

3. **Layer-cake pattern = the working state you want.** When headings/subheadings are visually distinct, descriptive, and consistent, users fixate on the headings and skip body text until they find the right section, then read the paragraph below it — "by far the most effective way to scan pages" short of full reading. This only works if subheadings are "descriptive of all topics in the section, and only topics in the section" and stand out "in a consistent, predictable manner." A layer-cake breaking back down into a pure F-sweep is the tell that headings aren't carrying enough visual or semantic weight. [source: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/layer-cake-pattern-scanning/] T1

4. **Spotted pattern** (skipping large chunks of text hunting for a specific token like a link, number, or price) is the third named eyetracking-verified pattern, distinct from F and layer-cake; it shows up when users are searching, not reading. Useful as a third diagnostic category when a heatmap doesn't match F or layer-cake. [source: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/f-shaped-pattern-reading-web-content/] T1

5. **Zigzag (alternating) image/text layouts actively degrade scanning efficiency for decorative imagery**, measured via eyetracking on 30-35 participants across real sites. Mechanism: the alternating position is "less predictable," causing "stumbling" — accidental fixations on images followed by a redirect back to text — and as users scroll, images "replace the text" mid-fixation. Fix: align decorative images consistently down the page (not alternating) and only use zigzag for 2-3 rows of purely informational imagery. This is a *named, measured* violation distinct from generic "clutter." [source: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/zigzag-page-layout/] T1

6. **"If you emphasize everything, nothing gets focus."** Nielsen's homepage-usability guideline (from the 113-guideline set, still cited as broadly valid) states this directly, plus the operational corollary: keep the number of high-priority tasks small (1-4) with a prominent location and "don't give them a lot of visual competition." This is the named-source version of "everything bold = nothing bold" — cite this exact phrase, not a paraphrase. [source: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/113-design-guidelines-homepage-usability/] CONV

7. **Squint test / blur test as a formal method.** NN/g runs this as a named technique: designers squint at (or blur) the screen to see what remains visually legible/prominent once text detail is lost; if the eye isn't drawn to the intended element under blur, the hierarchy is broken and needs rework. This is the operational version of "does it have ONE dominant focal point" — use it as a literal test step, not a metaphor. [source: https://www.nngroup.com/videos/squint-test/] CONV

8. **A screen supports roughly 2-3 hierarchy levels before it degrades**, per NN/g's visual-design principles: cap type scale at "no more than 3 sizes" (small/medium/large), restrict the "biggest/most important" treatment to a maximum of 2 large elements per screen, cap color to "2 primary and 2 secondary colors" with a max of "3 contrast variations" for complex designs. Also: "a maximum of two different font treatments in a single headline" (e.g. bold + one more, never bold + underline + color together — "it would compete with the bold treatment"). Use these as literal ceilings when auditing a screen's competing focal elements. [source: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/principles-visual-design/] [source: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/good-visual-design/] CONV

9. **Visual hierarchy operates on four contrast categories** per Wikipedia's synthesis of the design literature: color (hue/saturation/value contrast against surroundings), size, alignment/position (upper-left is seen first for Western readers; isolation via negative space increases prominence), and character/complexity (irregular or complex shapes draw more attention than simple/predictable ones). Order of importance emerges from which element has the *highest contrast to its surroundings* — not from any single channel being universally strongest; strength is always relative to what's around it, which is the underlying reason "everything bold" fails (removes the contrast that made bold work). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visual_hierarchy] CONV/T2

## What I could NOT verify and dropped

- A specific numeric ranking of "size vs weight vs color vs position vs isolation vs whitespace" by strength (e.g. "color beats size by X%") — no NN/g or primary source gives a validated numeric ordering; only "highest contrast to surroundings wins" (relative, not a fixed channel ranking) is sourced. Do not cite a channel-strength percentage anywhere.
- A specific NN/g-sourced "Z-pattern" eyetracking study — the Z-pattern is widely repeated in secondary UX-blog literature but I found no NN/g primary eyetracking article validating it the way the F-pattern and layer-cake pattern are validated (NN/g's own zigzag-layout research is actually critical of unpredictable Z/diagonal scanning for decorative images). Treat "Z-pattern" as an unverified folk convention, not equal-strength to F-pattern/layer-cake.
- Faces-as-entry-points / gaze-cueing research and a fixation-count-as-complexity-metric NN/g article — searched, no fetchable NN/g primary source found; dropped rather than softened.

## Diagnostic checklist (symptom → measure/check → named violation)

1. **Symptom:** "I can't tell what to look at first." → **Check:** run the squint/blur test (blur the screenshot or squint at the real screen) and note what's still visible. → **Violation:** no element wins the contrast-to-surroundings competition — broken focal hierarchy [source: https://www.nngroup.com/videos/squint-test/].

2. **Symptom:** "Everything on this screen feels loud / nothing pops." → **Check:** count how many elements simultaneously carry bold + color + large size at once. → **Violation:** "if you emphasize everything, nothing gets focus" [source: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/113-design-guidelines-homepage-usability/].

3. **Symptom:** Users only ever engage the top-left / top content, ignore the right side and lower sections. → **Check:** does the page rely on long unformatted paragraphs with no distinct headings? → **Violation:** default F-shaped skim triggered by "text that has little or no formatting for the web" [source: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/f-shaped-pattern-reading-web-content/].

4. **Symptom:** Users miss content under a subheading even though it's relevant to their task. → **Check:** do headings visually stand out "in a consistent, predictable manner" and accurately preview only their section's content? → **Violation:** collapsed layer-cake pattern — headings not carrying enough visual/semantic signal for scan-to-find behavior [source: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/layer-cake-pattern-scanning/].

5. **Symptom:** A decorative photo/illustration is stealing attention from the copy next to it, or users seem to "stumble" past it back to text. → **Check:** is the image alternating position (zigzag) relative to the text block, and is it decorative rather than informational? → **Violation:** zigzag unpredictability causing stray fixations [source: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/zigzag-page-layout/].

6. **Symptom:** Screen "feels busy" or has too many apparent priority tiers. → **Check:** count distinct type sizes and count elements at the "biggest/most important" tier. → **Violation:** exceeds the ~3-type-size / max-2-large-elements ceiling [source: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/principles-visual-design/].

7. **Symptom:** A single headline or label feels over-decorated. → **Check:** count simultaneous type treatments on one text run (bold + underline + color = 3). → **Violation:** exceeds "a maximum of two different font treatments in a single headline" [source: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/good-visual-design/].

8. **Symptom:** Hierarchy "looks fine" in isolation but users still can't complete the task efficiently. → **Check:** re-run the audit against the actual user task/goal, not the layout in the abstract (compare against a known low-fixation vs high-fixation task pattern). → **Violation:** hierarchy was tuned for reading, not for the task actually being performed — scanning is task-optimized, not fixed [source: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/eyetracking-tasks-efficient-scanning/].
