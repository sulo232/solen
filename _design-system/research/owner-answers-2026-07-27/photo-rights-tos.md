## Direct answer to the owner's question

No platform he named takes ownership. **All six take a licence, and every one of them says so explicitly.** Uber and Google go out of their way to say the content stays the user's. What those platforms actually take is a licence so broad it does everything ownership would do for a marketplace: worldwide, royalty-free, sublicensable, transferable, and expressly covering marketing. Solen should copy that pattern, not the assignment idea.

## 1. What each platform actually does

Quotes below are kept to minimal operative fragments (standard legal terms of art); everything else is paraphrased attribute-by-attribute.

### Airbnb — [Terms of Service §9 Content](https://www.airbnb.com/help/article/2908/terms-of-service)
- **Licence**, not assignment.
- Grant terms: non-exclusive, worldwide, royalty-free, perpetual, sub-licensable and transferable.
- Verbs: access, use, store, copy, modify, prepare derivative works, distribute, publish, transmit, stream, broadcast, "otherwise exploit".
- **Marketing named**: the stated purpose is to provide and/or promote the Airbnb Platform, "in particular on Internet and social networks" (Airbnb ToS §9).
- **Deletion/termination**: no clause reversing the licence on deletion. The grant is perpetual on its face.
- **Warranty**: yes, the user warrants they own the content or are authorised to grant the rights, and are liable for IP or privacy infringement.
- **Depicted-person consent**: not called out separately. This is Airbnb's weakest point and it is *not* the part to copy.

### Fresha — the direct competitor, and it has **two different regimes**
This is the single most useful finding.

**(a) [Terms of Use](https://terms.fresha.com/terms-use) — consumer/user submissions**
- Licence, not assignment. Fresha does not include a "you retain ownership" sentence.
- Grant: perpetual, irrevocable, worldwide, non-exclusive, royalty-free, fully sub-licensable.
- Marketing is not named in the grant; it's covered by the generic "in any form, media or technology".
- Moral rights handled as a **non-assertion**, not a waiver: the user "agree[s] not to assert any moral rights" (Fresha Terms of Use). That phrasing is deliberate and civil-law-correct, see section B.
- **No warranty of rights and no depicted-person consent requirement on the consumer side.**

**(b) [Partner Terms](https://terms.fresha.com/partner-terms) — the salon side**
- Licence: non-exclusive, royalty-free, irrevocable, worldwide, sublicensable to affiliates and third parties.
- **Explicit photo-consent duty**, and this is the clause Solen should model on. Fresha requires that where an uploaded photo, image or video "shows, relates to or may enable identification of any individual", the partner must obtain that individual's consent *before uploading*. Parent/guardian consent for minors. Partners must **keep consent records** and **remove content if consent is withdrawn**.
- Data-protection compliance for messaging/marketing is pushed onto the partner, with a 48-hour opt-out honouring window.
- **Where Fresha *does* take ownership**: content produced by Fresha's own paid photography service. Fresha owns those deliverables and licenses them back to the salon for use through Fresha services. That is the correct place for ownership: you own what you paid to create, you licence what someone uploads.

### Uber — [User Generated Content Policy](https://www.uber.com/legal/en/document/?name=user-generated-content-policy&country=united-states&lang=en)
- Licence. Uber states plainly that UGC remains the user's property.
- Grant: worldwide, perpetual, irrevocable, transferable, sub-licensable, royalty-free.
- **Drafting outlier worth noting**: I checked the grant sentence word for word and the words "exclusive" and "non-exclusive" do **not** appear anywhere in it. Everyone else says non-exclusive. Silence here is a drafting gap, not a model to copy.
- Scope: "all formats and distribution channels now known or hereafter devised", including third-party sites, without further notice or consent and without payment.
- **Warranty**: the user represents they have "all rights, licences, consents and releases necessary". "Consents and releases" is doing the depicted-person work implicitly, but it is not spelled out.
- No deletion carve-out.

### Booking.com — two layers, and the guest-facing one has the best plain-English wording anywhere
**(a) [Terms & Conditions §A15.4](https://www.booking.com/content/terms.html)** — guest reviews and pictures. Licence, worded as a user confirmation rather than a formal grant: by uploading, you confirm Booking.com may use it on the platform and "for any other commercial purposes (including in a promotional context), everywhere, forever" (Booking.com T&Cs A15.4). Marketing is named. The user confirms the content is truthful, that they have permission to share it, and that no privacy rights are violated, and accepts liability for claims.

**(b) [General Delivery Terms §3](https://admin.booking.com/hotelreg/terms-and-conditions.html?cc1=01&lang=en)** — partner side. Non-exclusive, royalty-free, worldwide licence over Accommodation Information and IP, expressly sublicensable to affiliates and strategic partners; Booking.com runs marketing campaigns using the property name (§4.3.1). Partner warrants accuracy and completeness.

### Treatwell — the closest comparable (European beauty booking)
[Partner Terms of Business](https://www.treatwell.co.uk/info/supplier-terms-and-conditions/)
- Licence: non-exclusive, royalty-free, irrevocable, worldwide, sublicensable.
- **Marketing is enumerated by channel, not left generic**: the Marketplace, Treatwell's own social media pages, Distribution Channels, Reserve with Google, "any other medium used by Treatwell", plus SEO and pay-per-click using the partner's trading name.
- **Survives termination**: the licence remains in effect after the agreement ends.
- **Warranty**: the partner must have "full copyright and permission to publicly display them indefinitely for any purpose" (Treatwell Partner Terms).
- **Depicted-person consent, with record-keeping**: the partner must obtain every relevant individual's consent to be advertised on the Marketplace including for photos, and keep accurate records of those consents.

Treatwell and Fresha's partner terms converge on the same answer, from opposite ends of Europe. That convergence is the strongest signal in this research.

### Yelp — [Terms of Service §5](https://terms.yelp.com/tos/en_us/)
- Licence, expressly irrevocably granted: world-wide, perpetual, non-exclusive, royalty-free, assignable, sublicensable, transferable, "to use Your Content for any purpose" (Yelp ToS §5.2). Section 5.3 confirms that as between the parties, the user owns the content.
- **Advertising named literally**: Yelp may incorporate content into advertisements and other works.
- **Moral rights waived**, US-style: users irrevocably waive claims and assertions of moral rights or attribution.
- **Deletion**: §5.1 warns content cannot always be withdrawn once posted; the perpetual licence survives.

### Google Business Profile — [Google Terms of Service](https://policies.google.com/terms)
- The most restrained of the six, and deliberately so. Google states: your content remains yours, and Google won't claim ownership.
- Grant: worldwide, non-exclusive, royalty-free.
- **Purpose-limited**, which is the key difference: operating and improving the services, using publicly shared content to promote the services, and developing new services. It is not "for any purpose".
- Sublicensing is limited to other users (so the product works) and to contractors under back-to-back terms.
- **Duration tied to the content, plus a real deletion path**: the licence lasts as long as the content is protected by IP rights, and on removal Google stops making it publicly available within a reasonable time, with caveats for already-shared copies and search indexing.

## A. Is assignment normal? No. And demanding it costs more than it buys.

Zero of the six take assignment of uploaded content. The industry standard is unambiguous: **broad non-exclusive licence + user keeps ownership.** The only place ownership appears is Fresha taking ownership of photos its own photographers produced under a paid service, which is a completely different transaction.

Practical downsides of demanding assignment:

1. **It buys nothing Solen needs.** Everything the owner listed (ads, social, SEO, banners, out-of-home, resizing, cropping, third-party channels) is achievable with a licence. Ownership only adds the ability to *sue third parties* over the salon's photo and to *stop the salon* from using its own picture. Solen wants neither. Blocking a salon from posting its own work on Instagram is a support nightmare and a churn driver.

2. **Business-customer pushback is the real cost.** Salons that hired a photographer, or that use a chain's brand assets, frequently *cannot* assign, because they only hold a licence themselves. An assignment clause makes the warranty unsatisfiable, which means either every salon breaches on signature or they refuse to sign. A licence with a sublicence-through right is satisfiable.

3. **Enforceability, Switzerland-specific**: see B. An assignment buried in click-through T&Cs is the textbook "unusual clause".

4. **It makes Solen the liable party.** Owning content pulls Solen toward primary liability for what the picture shows. A licence plus an indemnity from the uploader puts the risk where the knowledge is.

5. **It doesn't survive contact with the actual risk.** The thing that will actually bite Solen is not copyright, it is the *client in the photo*. Assignment gives Solen precisely zero protection there, because the client never signed anything with Solen. Section C is the real problem; assignment is a distraction from it.

## B. Swiss law on assignment and moral rights

**Copyright is transferable in Switzerland.** [Art. 16(1) URG](https://www.rechtundgesetz.ch/15_76_314_URG_gesetzestexte_artikel_16_Art_16_Rechtsuebergang.html): "Das Urheberrecht ist übertragbar und vererblich." So an assignment is not void per se. But four things constrain a blanket, future-facing assignment sitting inside standard T&Cs:

1. **Art. 16(2) URG — no bundling by implication.** Transferring one partial right does not carry the others unless expressly agreed. Sloppy drafting is read *against* the grantee.

2. **Zweckübertragungstheorie (purpose-of-transfer doctrine).** Derived from good faith under Art. 2 ZGB: in case of doubt, the author is taken to have granted only the scope the contract's purpose actually requires. A vague "everything uploaded is Solen's" therefore risks being cut back judicially to "whatever Solen needed for the listing", which is *less* than a well-drafted explicit licence would have given. Demanding too much can leave you with less. ([Zweckübertragungslehre](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zweck%C3%BCbertragungslehre))

3. **Art. 27(2) ZGB — übermässige Bindung.** Nobody may surrender their freedom or restrict its use to a degree offending law or morality. A perpetual, irrevocable, unlimited assignment of *all future* works by a salon is the classic shape courts test here; excessive duration typically yields a termination right rather than full nullity.

4. **The Ungewöhnlichkeitsregel is the sharpest edge, and it applies B2B.** Where T&Cs are adopted globally without being read clause by clause (Globalübernahme, which is presumed, and the *user* of the T&Cs bears the burden of proving the customer knew of the disputed clause), unusual clauses that the consenting party could not reasonably have expected are ineffective. Crucially, unlike the content control in **Art. 8 UWG — which protects consumers only, not businesses** — the Ungewöhnlichkeitsregel is not excluded in B2B settings. A copyright assignment hidden in a booking-platform signup is exactly the clause a Swiss salon "could not reasonably expect". ([HSLU Management and Law Blog](https://hub.hslu.ch/management-and-law/2023/11/07/agb-recht-ungewoehnliche-anwendung-der-ungewoehnlichkeitsregel/), [Stach Rechtsanwälte on Art. 8 UWG](https://stach.ch/die-generell-abstrakte-pruefung-inhaltskontrolle-von-agb-nach-art-8-uwg/))

   Note the asymmetry for Solen's two audiences: against **consumers** (clients uploading review photos) Art. 8 UWG *also* applies, so a perpetual + irrevocable grant from a consumer is the single most exposed clause in the whole document.

**Moral rights (Urheberpersönlichkeitsrecht) cannot be assigned away.** Art. 9 URG (recognition of authorship, control over first publication) and Art. 11 URG (integrity of the work) are personality rights. Art. 11(2) URG sets an absolute limit: even where a third party is contractually or legally entitled to modify the work, the author may still oppose a distortion that violates their personality. A US-style "user irrevocably waives all moral rights" clause, like Yelp's, is not reliably effective in Switzerland.

**The workaround is Fresha's, and Solen should use it:** an obligation *not to assert* moral rights (pactum de non petendo) plus an express consent to specific, named modifications, rather than a blanket waiver. So: "you agree Solen may crop, resize, colour-correct, compress, watermark and overlay text, and you agree not to assert moral rights in respect of those uses" — which names the modifications, so the consent is specific, and frames the residue as non-assertion.

## C. The data-protection chain — this is the actual risk, not copyright

The salon owns (or licenses) the copyright. The **client owns the personality right in their own face**, and no contract between Solen and the salon can transfer that. Three separate legal layers apply to one photo:

**1. Art. 28 ZGB — Recht am eigenen Bild.** As soon as a person is recognisable in the foreground, or the image is to be used commercially, that person's consent is mandatory. For advertising use specifically, Swiss doctrine requires **express consent covering the concrete intended use — a general or blanket consent is not sufficient**, and a consent given once does not extend to other purposes, other users, or future uses. Consent is also revocable going forward. ([PBM Avocats](https://www.pbm-avocats.ch/de/recht-am-bild-schweiz/), [Schweizerische Kriminalprävention, "Das eigene Bild"](https://www.skppsc.ch/de/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2016/12/rechteigenesbild.pdf))

This is decisive for the owner's question: **a term in Solen's T&Cs, no matter how broad, cannot manufacture the client's consent.** The salon's promise is a contractual allocation of risk between Solen and the salon; it is not a legal basis for Solen's own advertising use.

**2. nFADP / revDSG.** [Art. 5 lit. c](https://datenschutz.law/revdsg/2-kapitel/1-abschnitt-begriffe-und-grundsaetze/art-5) defines besonders schützenswerte Personendaten to include health data, data on the private sphere, genetic data, and biometric data that uniquely identifies a person. [Art. 6(6)](https://datenschutz.law/revdsg/2-kapitel/1-abschnitt-begriffe-und-grundsaetze/art-6): consent is valid only if given voluntarily for one or more specific processing operations after adequate information. **Art. 6(7): consent must be *ausdrücklich* (explicit) for processing sensitive personal data**, and explicit means expressed in words or an unambiguous signal, never silence or tacit agreement.

An ordinary photo is **not** automatically sensitive data: it becomes biometric only when processed through a technical means enabling unique identification. Same position as [GDPR Recital 51](https://gdpr-info.eu/recitals/no-51/), which says processing photographs should not systematically be treated as special-category processing.

**3. Where a beauty photo *does* tip into sensitive data.** This is the part specific to Solen's vertical and it is not theoretical. A photo or its caption can reveal health data (alopecia, scalp psoriasis, eczema, post-chemo hair, a patch-test result, a scar being covered), ethnic origin (afro/textured hair services), or religious belief (a hijab, sheitel, or a modesty-related service). Practitioner guidance in this industry is explicit that the moment you record an allergy, skin condition, patch test or medical note affecting treatment, you are in Article 9 territory and legitimate interests is unavailable — you need explicit consent. Before/after photos in particular are treated as requiring separate written consent naming each channel, with withdrawal available at any time. ([LegalVision UK](https://legalvision.co.uk/data-privacy-it/gdpr-beauty-salon-business/), [ComplianceFix](https://compliancefix.co.uk/blog/gdpr-hair-beauty-salons))

**Who is controller?** When Solen puts a client's photo in a Solen ad, Solen is deciding purpose and means. Solen is a **controller for that use**, not a processor of the salon's data. So Solen needs its own lawful basis and must be **named in the consent the client gave**. A consent form that says "I allow [Salon] to use my photo on its social media" does not cover a Solen Instagram ad.

**The consent chain that actually works:**
1. Salon obtains the client's explicit, informed, documented consent **before** upload.
2. That consent must name Solen (or "the booking platform the salon lists on, including for the platform's own advertising") and must enumerate the channels: Solen app and website, Solen social accounts, paid social, search ads, print, partner sites.
3. Salon confirms this at upload time, per photo or per batch.
4. Salon keeps the record and produces it on request.
5. Withdrawal propagates: salon notifies Solen, Solen pulls the photo from live surfaces and from future campaigns within a stated window.
6. Solen's own privacy notice discloses that salon-uploaded imagery may be used in Solen marketing, and gives the depicted person a direct route to object without going through the salon.

Step 6 is the one platforms usually skip and it is the one that makes the chain defensible, because the data subject has no contract with Solen and must be able to reach Solen directly.

## D. Upload-time attestation — real examples

The best-performing pattern in practice is a short confirmation at the moment of upload, because that produces a timestamped record tied to the specific file, whereas a T&Cs clause produces nothing.

Real examples found:

- **Booking.com** puts the whole thing at upload rather than in a grant clause: by uploading a review or picture you are confirming you have permission to share it, it violates nobody's privacy rights, and Booking.com may use it commercially including promotionally, everywhere, forever ([T&Cs A15.4](https://www.booking.com/content/terms.html)).
- **Etsy**: by uploading images, you confirm you have all necessary rights to those photos and are complying with the IP policy ([Listing Image Requirements](https://www.etsy.com/legal/policy/listing-image-requirements/253962679005)).
- **Tripadvisor** uses an actual checkbox for business owner photos: you tick a box confirming you are a representative of the business and that you have the rights to post the photo ([Business owner photo guidelines](https://www.tripadvisorsupport.com/en-US/hc/owner/articles/416)).
- **Treatwell** and **Fresha partner terms** both require the *consent record*, not just the assertion, which is what turns an attestation into evidence.

**Design guidance that came out of the research and matters here:** don't bundle. If you need a stronger release for content you'll actually feature in advertising, trigger it as a **separate** control rather than hiding it in the same checkbox as "I agree to upload" — that produces a cleaner record and defeats the argument that consent was bundled too broadly. This is also exactly what Swiss law demands: consent to advertising use must be specific, and a blanket one doesn't count.

## RECOMMENDED CLAUSE OUTLINE FOR SOLEN

Four pieces, in three different places. Not one mega-clause.

### 1. Salon Terms — Content licence (business side)
- Salon **keeps ownership**. Say this in the first sentence; it removes the main objection and costs Solen nothing.
- Salon grants Solen a **non-exclusive, worldwide, royalty-free, transferable, sub-licensable licence** to host, store, reproduce, adapt (**enumerate**: crop, resize, colour-correct, compress, watermark, overlay text), publish, distribute and communicate the content.
- **Purposes, enumerated by channel** (copy Treatwell's specificity, not Yelp's "for any purpose"): the Solen app and website; Solen's own social media accounts; paid advertising on social and search; email and push marketing; SEO/PPC using the salon's trading name; press and partner distribution channels; and offline/print.
- **Sub-licence to affiliates, contractors and distribution partners under back-to-back terms** (Google's contractor limitation is the cleaner model than an unbounded sublicence).
- **Duration and deletion — do not copy Airbnb/Fresha/Yelp here.** Say: the licence for *live surfaces* ends when the salon deletes the content or the account closes, Solen removing it within a stated period (e.g. 30 days). A **narrow survival**: copies already embedded in campaigns, print runs, cached or archived material may persist, with a defined wind-down (e.g. no *new* placements after removal; existing placements retired within 90 days). This is far more defensible than "perpetual and irrevocable" and is the single biggest deviation I recommend from what the competitors do.
- **Moral rights**: express consent to the enumerated modifications + an agreement not to assert moral rights in respect of those uses. **Not** a blanket waiver.
- **Attribution**: state whether Solen credits the salon. Recommend yes by default; it partially satisfies Art. 9 URG and is good for the salon.

### 2. Salon Terms — Warranties, consent duty, indemnity
- Salon warrants it owns or holds all rights necessary to grant the above, including any photographer's licence with onward-sublicence rights.
- **Where a photo shows or may enable identification of any individual**, the salon must obtain that person's explicit, informed, documented consent **before uploading**, covering use by Solen including Solen's own advertising in the enumerated channels. Parent/guardian consent for minors.
- Salon must **keep the consent records**, produce them within a stated period on request, and **notify Solen without undue delay** of any withdrawal.
- Salon must not upload images revealing health conditions, treatment records, or other sensitive data unless it holds explicit consent specifically covering that.
- **Indemnity** for third-party claims arising from uploaded content, and Solen's right to remove any content at its discretion without justification.
- **Provide a Solen-branded consent template** the salon can hand to clients. This is the highest-leverage item in the whole document: it is the difference between a warranty that is technically breached by 90% of salons and one they can actually comply with.

### 3. Client (consumer) Terms — review photos
- Same structure, deliberately narrower, because Art. 8 UWG applies to consumers:
- Non-exclusive, worldwide, royalty-free licence; **not** perpetual, **not** irrevocable.
- On deletion, Solen ceases new public and marketing use within a stated period.
- Marketing use of consumer photos should be **opt-in**, separately, not swept in by the general licence.
- Client confirms the photo is theirs, that everyone identifiable in it agreed, and that it does not violate anyone's privacy.

### 4. Upload-time UI (the enforcement layer, and where this actually gets won)
Three separate controls, never one:
- **Checkbox 1, always**: "I have the rights to this photo." Blocking.
- **Checkbox 2, conditional on the salon indicating the photo shows a person**: "The person shown gave me permission for this photo to be used by Solen, including in Solen advertising. I have a record of it." Blocking. Link to the Solen consent template next to it.
- **Toggle 3, separate and off by default**: "Solen may feature this photo in Solen advertising campaigns." Not blocking, opt-in.

Store per upload: user id, timestamp, IP, the exact wording version shown, and each control's value. That log is the evidence. The T&Cs clause is not.

### Where a lawyer must sign off (do not ship any of these on my analysis alone)
1. **Duration and survival wording.** The trade-off between competitor-standard "perpetual + irrevocable" and my recommended deletion-plus-wind-down is a commercial and legal judgment call, and it interacts with Art. 27(2) ZGB and the Ungewöhnlichkeitsregel. A lawyer decides how far to push.
2. **Controller vs. processor allocation** for client photos, and whether a joint-controller arrangement (Art. 26 GDPR) is required for EU-resident clients. This determines whether Solen needs a data-processing annex or a joint-controller arrangement with every salon.
3. **Whether Solen's verticals put hair/skin photos into sensitive-data territory** as a routine matter, which would move the whole regime to mandatory explicit consent under Art. 6(7) nDSG and Art. 9 GDPR. My read is that plain styling photos are not sensitive, but scalp, skin, medical-aesthetic and any allergy/patch-test-adjacent content are. A lawyer draws the line.
4. **The consent template's wording**, since it is the document Solen's whole advertising chain rests on, and Swiss law requires it be specific to the concrete use rather than blanket.
5. **Indemnity scope and cap**, and whether an uncapped IP/privacy indemnity against small salons survives the Ungewöhnlichkeitsregel.
6. **The moral-rights non-assertion clause**, which is the civil-law construct with the least settled Swiss case law of anything here.
7. **Four-language parity.** The clause must carry the same legal meaning in DE, FR, IT and EN; the Ungewöhnlichkeitsregel bites hardest on a clause that is clear in one locale and buried in another.

### One thing to tell the owner plainly
The instinct behind "all pictures uploaded are mine" is right about the *outcome* he wants (Solen can use salon photos in ads freely) and wrong about the *mechanism*. Ownership does not get him there, because the person who can actually stop the ad is the client in the chair, and she never signed Solen's terms. The competitor that solved this best is Fresha's partner terms and Treatwell's, and both solved it with a licence plus a **pre-upload consent duty with record-keeping** — not with ownership.
